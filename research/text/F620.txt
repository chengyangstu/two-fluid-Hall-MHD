1.  Introduction
In typical space plasmas such as in the solar wind the Hall term in the generalized Ohm's law is near zero. 
This is because the ions and electrons both remain magnetized in a plasma without small-scale gradients. 
However, near the reconnecting magnetopause within ion diffusion regions the Hall term can be non-zero (Mozer 
et al., 2002, 2008). Non-zero Hall electric fields also may occur near the lunar surface (Jarvinen et al., 2014; 
Saito et al., 2012). Near the lunar surface the scale size of the crustal magnetic field is smaller than that of the ion 
gyroradius and thus the ions are not capable of responding to the small-scale Lorentz forces that are developed, 
while the electrons remain magnetized and respond accordingly.
Saito et al., 2012 suggested that if the ions were demagnetized near the lunar surface while the electrons were still 
magnetized, the resulting Hall electric field could explain the simultaneous ion deceleration and electron accel-
eration observed near crustal magnetic fields. However, the Saito et al., 2012 study does not explicitly show the 
demagnetized ions, but rather infers the presence of the Hall electric field. Jarvinen et al., 2014 further showed 
the generation of a Hall electric field by the demagnetization of ions with use of a 3-D quasi-neutral hybrid 
simulation with the ions as particles and the electrons acting as a charge neutralizing fluid. The results suggest 
that the Hall electric field is directly associated with lunar crustal magnetic fields. These two studies indicate that 
the near lunar surface environment exhibits an important characteristic, the ions are demagnetized—yet no direct 
evidence has been reported up to date.

solar wind IMF and lunar crustal magnetic fields. Simulation and modeling studies (Deca et al., 2015; Giacalone 
Abstract  The near lunar surface contains small-scale magnetic field structures that provide a natural test 
bed for observing plasmas with a non-zero Hall electric field, as well as potentially facilitating electron-only 
reconnection. This study presents observational evidence of magnetized electrons as well as demagnetized 
ions when THEMIS-ARTEMIS probe B reached an altitude of ∼15 km above the lunar surface. Additionally, 
observations suggest the presence of a field line topology change and traversal of a closed magnetic field 
structure containing solar wind electrons, suggestive of magnetic reconnection having occurred at some point 
between the solar wind interplanetary magnetic field and a lunar crustal magnetic field. Thus, the observations 
presented here are consistent with previous studies that predict prominent Hall electric fields near lunar crustal 
magnetic fields and further suggest that the solar wind interplanetary magnetic field may reconnect with lunar 
crustal magnetic fields, most likely via electron-only reconnection.
Plain Language Summary  While interactions between the solar wind and the Earth's 
magnetosphere have been well studied, there is still much to be learned by studying the interactions between the 
solar wind and the small-scale lunar magnetic fields. Due to the small-scale nature of the lunar magnetic fields, 
previous studies have suggested that the ions do not respond in the same manner as the electrons. The resulting 
effects lead to an electric field near regions of lunar magnetic fields. This study presents observational evidence 
of the aforementioned phenomena. Additionally, the spacecraft observations also suggest that magnetic 
reconnection, or the breaking of the lunar magnetic field lines and reconnection to the magnetic field in the 
solar wind, was occurring between the solar wind and the lunar magnetic fields.

fields. As a result, the exploration of reconnection with in-situ orbiting space-
craft near the lunar surface is of considerable interest.
The focus of this study is to assess whether reconnection may have occurred 
between the IMF and lunar crustal magnetic fields, and determine if the envi-
ronment supports the demagnetization of ions in the near lunar surface envi-
ronment. In order to accomplish these tasks, we utilized in-situ electron and 
ion plasma observations as well as electric and magnetic field measurements 
made by the THEMIS-ARTEMIS spacecraft in the near lunar surface envi-
ronment, during an orbit when the spacecraft reached an altitude at periselene 
near 15 km on 04 December 2013.
2.  THEMIS-ARTEMIS Instrumentation and Observations
This study presents an event from 04 December 2013 when the THEMIS-ARTEMIS Probe B (THB) made 
near-lunar surface measurements at altitudes of 10s of km near the terminator. THEMIS-ARTEMIS Probe C 
(THC) was in the solar wind and acted as a near moon solar wind monitor for the duration of the event. The orbits 
of the spacecraft are shown in Figure 1 along with the approximate IMF projection on the X-Y plane. The X-Z 
plane is not shown as THB was in a primarily equatorial orbit.
We utilized data from the ion and electron top-hat electrostatic analyzers (ESA) (McFadden et al., 2008), electric 
field double probes (EFI) (Bonnell et al., 2008), and the fluxgate magnetometer (FGM) (Auster et al., 2008). 
Electron burst mode data was available from the ESA, allowing for approximately 4 s time steps and full energy 
and solid angle coverage. We utilized the DC electric field measurements from the EFI double probes. Only 
data from the spin-plane probes were used, while data from the axial probe was disregarded due to uncertainties 
resulting from the shorter probe length and the probe shorting factor (see Bonnell et al., 2008 for details on this 
error source). Additionally, the spin-plane probe measurements required a constant offset voltage. We applied 
a 2.5 mV/m offset from 00:08:00. The 2.5 mV/m offset was determined from the typical offsets found in the 
Bonnell et al., 2008 study. We further increased the offset during the solar wind interval to 4.5 mV/m in order 
to make the E × B velocity zero within the solar wind. The FGM data utilized in this study provided the full 
3D-vector magnetic field. The data from THC was shifted in time to match the data from THB. This was accom-
plished by aligning structures in the solar wind that were observed by both spacecraft.

3.  Results and Discussion
From Figures 2 and 3 we draw two results: (a) the closed field line topology contains electrons with a similar 
energy distribution to that of the solar wind. (b) The electrons remain magnetized, while the ions are demagnetized.
The first result from this event comes from the traversal of the spacecraft onto a closed magnetic field topology. 
During the traversal of the region marked “closed” in Figure 2 the electron pitch angle distributions indicated 
a double-sided loss cone distribution. This distribution indicates that both footpoints of the magnetic field line 
passing through the spacecraft are embedded in the lunar regolith. As a result, the electrons are mirrored near

To observe a closed field line structure with solar wind electrons, one possibility is that an open field line 
structure underwent a topology change to become closed. For this to occur, reconnection must have happened 
between the solar wind IMF and a lunar crustal magnetic field at some point on the lunar surface. It is unlikely 
that the observed solar wind electrons could simply gyrate onto the closed field line structure. In an ∼10 nT 
field, the gyroradius of a 10 eV electron is ∼1 km. The closed field line structure is traversed in ∼20 s leading to 
an encounter of the closed field line region which was ∼40 km in length, an order of magnitude larger than the 
electron gyroradius. Additionally, the vertical thickness of the open region prior to the traversal of the closed field 
line region corresponded to a vertical thickness of ∼35 km, also an order of magnitude larger than the electron 
gyroradius. Therefore, the solar wind electrons on the closed field line structure are potentially afforded access 
to the region through reconnection. Figure 4 shows a schematic of a possible reconnection geometry and space-
craft trajectory. An alternate possibility is that secondary electron emission from the surface could give rise to an 
electron population trapped on a closed field line structure. This scenario would therefore not require magnetic 
reconnection to take place. However, while secondary electron emission may be occurring it is unlikely that the 
solar wind electrons would be observed throughout the structure as the electron gyroradius was much less than 
the observed closed field line structure thickness. Therefore, magnetic reconnection is required to explain the 
solar wind electron populations present.
On further inspection of the structure of the velocity distributions in Figure 3 and the perpendicular electron 
temperature profile in Figure 2i, the electron distributions in the perpendicular plane appear mostly gyrotropic. 
There are no obvious crescent shaped features indicative of an agyrotropic electron distribution within an elec-
tron diffusion region. Additionally, the similarity in the perpendicular electron temperature profiles, as shown 
in Figure 2i, further support the gyrotropic nature of the electron distributions. Since there is no significant 
agyrotropy, as expected near the separatrix or the electron diffusion region., it is likely that either a) the electron 
diffusion region is not near the spacecraft location and so we do not observe any finite gyroradius effects or b) 
more likely the temporal and therefore spatial resolution of the THEMIS-ARTEMIS spacecraft is inadequate to 
resolve the finite gyroradius effects that drive the agyrotropic electron distributions.
The second result comes from the Vi,z and Ve,z measurements. From Figure 2g, Vi,z and Ve,z are not equal outside 
of the solar wind interval. Some disagreement between Vi,z and Ve,z could result from errors related to the diffi-
culty in determining the bulk electron velocity from the electron distributions. However, comparison of Vi,z and 
Ve,z with the (E × B)z velocity suggests a different interpretation. There is agreement between Ve,z and (E × B)z, 
supporting the result that the electrons are magnetized. On the other hand, the lack of a response in Vi,z indicates 
that the ions are demagnetized within this region. One of the key results of the Saito et al. (2012) and Jarvinen 
et al. (2014) studies depended on the ions becoming demagnetized near the lunar surface, thus generating the 
Hall electric field proposed by both studies. Our second result provides experimental evidence of the proposed 
Hall  electric field in these studies. The results presented here showcase a very similar environment to that found 
near the magnetopause by Mozer et al., 2008. That study found that there was a region near the reconnecting

Mozer et al. (2008) showed that the electric field measured by the EFI instrument agreed with the Hall electric 
field. This study presents a very similar result near the lunar surface. When Ve,z is consistent with (E × B)z, we 
can utilize the bulk ion and electron velocities to calculate the Hall electric field from the plasma data (i.e., 
J = Vi − Ve). Figure 2h shows a correspondence between the E + Vi × B and the Hall electric field, consistent with 
the differential motion between the ions and the electrons generating a Hall electric field within this region. The 
magnitude of the calculated Hall electric field is shown in Figure 5 over a map of the local crustal field magni-
tude. Figure 5 shows that the Hall electric field magnitude is enhanced near regions of relatively higher local 
crustal field strength. This result qualitatively agrees with previous predictions of Hall electric fields driven by 
differential motion between ions and electrons at crustal fields (Jarvinen et al., 2014; Saito et al., 2012). However, 
from Figure 2d there is no discernible ion deceleration and Figure 2i suggests electron heating as opposed to 
acceleration. The lack of ion deceleration could be due to the fact that this event was over a particularly weak 
crustal magnetic field compared to the event described in Saito et al., 2012.
From the results presented in this study it is most likely that the reconnection process at play would be electron-only 
reconnection (Phan et al., 2018; Stawarz et al., 2019, 2022). This assertion is driven by the fact that the small scale 
size of the crustal field regions may provide a natural regime for electron-scale current sheets to form that are not 
embedded in a larger ion-scale current sheet. During the electron-only reconnection events exhibited in previous 
studies, no modification of the ion dynamics were reported. Thus, the lack of ion acceleration, as expected in 
traditional reconnection, would be accounted for in this scenario. As a result, the region in the near lunar surface 
environment may naturally cause the resulting plasma regime to be in a state where the ions do not participate in 
the reconnection process.
4.  Conclusions
In conclusion, the THEMIS-ARTEMIS observations described in this study reveal the following characteristics:
1.	 The closed field line topology containing solar wind electrons may indicate the occurrence of reconnection at 
some point between the solar wind IMF and a lunar crustal magnetic field.
2.	 The region near the lunar surface contains demagnetized ions and magnetized electrons that are E × B drifting, 
resulting in a Hall electric field associated with regions of relatively stronger crustal magnetic field strength.
3.	 Given the demagnetized nature of the ions and the relevant spatial scale being a small fraction of an ion iner-
tial length, it is most likely that the type of reconnection that occurs in the near lunar surface environment is 
electron-only reconnection.

Given the above conclusions we can state that the near lunar surface environment can support the demagneti-
zation of ions as previously predicted. From the observed closed magnetic field topology containing solar wind 
electrons we posit that magnetic reconnection may have occurred at some point between the IMF and a lunar 
crustal magnetic field. Future work and low-altitude measurements at a higher temporal resolution are needed 
to explore further the nature of the reconnection process in this environment. For instance, what are the factors 
of import that can suppress (Doss et al., 2015, 2016; Swisdak et al., 2003) magnetic reconnection in the plasma 
regime found near lunar crustal magnetic fields? Given the new interest in electron-only reconnection, it is of 
great interest to the community to utilize the Moon as a natural laboratory to explore the fundamental nature of 
reconnection.
