[A] Key results 
This paper provides a unified interpretation of the bifurcated current sheets observed in laboratory experiments, particle
simulations, and satellite observations in the Earth's magnetotail and heliospheric current sheets. In particular, the authors’
idea that a narrow layer with large potential gradient due to the two-fluid effect could replace the discontinuous shock
surface assumed in the Petschek model can be evaluated as a major step forward in solving the problem of collisionless fast
magnetic reconnection. I strongly hope that the authors will improve their manuscript based on the following comments. 
[B] Validity 
The validity of each data interpretation will be discussed in the following sections, but the results are generally considered to
be valid, except for Fig 6. A significant change (sudden bending of the separatrices) is observed in z>95di of Fig. 6a, which
is unlikely to be continuous with the 20di<z<80di region. If the distance z~100di has any physical meaning, please explain it.
Also, please make sure it is not a problem of the simulation technique (e.g., computational domain). 
[C] Significance 
The conclusions of this paper bring about a major change in the focus when dealing with magnetic reconnection
phenomena close to antiparallel conditions. I believe that their research results will have a significant impact not only on the
field of magnetic reconnection research, but also on a broader range of fields including plasma physics, space physics,
astrophysics, and nuclear fusion science.

experiments, simulations, and satellite observations. But please indicate what each variable in the simulation is normalized

[D3] Jy in MMS 
On line 394, the authors state that significant Ex and Jy were observed simultaneously, as indicated by the three gray-box

Response to Reviewers’ Comments 
Reviewer 1  
We thank the reviewer for valuable comments. The review helped us recognize that several 
central points of the manuscript, especially the distinction between EBCS, the separatrix region, 
and the broader exhaust, as well as the intended meaning of “universal” and “quasi-steady”, 
were not presented with sufficient precision. In the revised manuscript, we have therefore 
clarified the definition of EBCS, distinguished common boundary-layer features from 
environment-specific kinetic activity, modified several statements, and added a discussion to 
better explain both the similarities and differences among the laboratory, magnetotail, and 
heliospheric current-sheet cases. 
 
 
1.​  However, the features along separatrix region during reconnection in solar wind and in 
the magnetosphere are dramatically different. In the magnetosphere, enhanced plasma 
waves, especially electrostatic waves, strong parallel electric field structure, plasma 
density peak/cavity, temperature decreases, intense electron beam parallel to the 
magnetic field, and strong parallel current are generally observed. Of course, the 
sub-alfvenic ion jets are bounded by the separatrix regions. Some of these features are 
consistent with EBCS in this paper. On the other hand, in the solar wind, a pair of the 
slow-mode shocks or discontinuities are detected in the boundary of 
reconnection-related sub-alfvenic ion jets which has been thought to be quasi-steady 
reconnection. In this paper, the separatrix region are called EBCS. However, the features 
along the EBCS in the solar wind are quite different from those in the magnetosphere. 
For example, in the solar wind, the sharp magnetic field gradient regarded as current 
layer was detected, without enhanced waves, electron beams etc. So, this reviewer does 
not think the authors have found many common features along the boundary of the 
reconnection outflow jets. ​
​
We thank the reviewer for raising this point, which touches upon a central theme of our 
paper that we now realize requires clearer explanation. We believe that the apparent 
discrepancy in features between the solar wind (HCS) and the magnetosphere is not due 
to a fundamental difference in separatrix physics, but is primarily the result of (1) the vast 
difference in macroscopic system sizes relative to the boundary width, and (2) historical 
limitations in instrumental temporal resolution.​
 
First, regarding the 'sharpness' of the boundary and the slow-shock interpretation: In the 
solar wind (PSP event), the total exhaust width is large (~ 4000di). Therefore, an EBCS 
boundary that is kinetically wide (~ 20di) appears as an extremely sharp, step-like 
transition in macroscopic 1D profiles. Because there is such a vast scale separation, this 
boundary has thus historically been classified purely as an MHD slow-mode shock. In 
contrast, in the MMS magnetotail event, the entire exhaust width is ~20di. Consequently, 
the boundary layers occupy a large fraction of the exhaust, making the gradients appear 

=== PAGE 6 ===
less 'sudden' relative to the overall system size. However, our data demonstrate that the 
underlying two-fluid structure is similar in  terms of two-fluid physics.​
​
Second, we agree with the reviewer’s insight, supported by Wang et al. (2023), that the 
central X-line itself can be bursty and turbulent. However, as the outflow propagates, the 
extended exhaust boundaries (EBCS) maintain a macroscopically coherent structure 
over large distances. It is precisely this macroscopic stability that has historically led to 
the 'steady slow-shock' interpretation in the solar wind. 
Finally, regarding the perceived absence of enhanced waves, electron beams, and 
parallel electric fields in the solar wind: This 'absence' is largely an artifact of spacecraft 
measurement cadences that cannot resolve kinetic-scale fluctuations. When sufficient 
resolution is available, kinetic separatrix features are indeed observed in the solar wind. 
For instance, recent Parker Solar Probe observations by Phan et al. (2022) directly 
identified energetic proton beams leaking along separatrix field lines outside the 
heliospheric current sheet (HCS). Additionally, high-cadence waveform captures have 
revealed intense electrostatic waves localized to solar wind current sheets (Malaspina et 
al., 2013). While the parallel electric field is difficult to resolve directly in the solar wind, 
its existence is physically required to support the potential well that drives the observed 
ion acceleration. 
A primary conclusion of our paper is that what has historically been interpreted as a 
macro-scale 'slow-shock' in the solar wind can be unified with magnetospheric and 
laboratory observations through the two-fluid kinetic framework of EBCS. We have 
revised the Discussion section to explicitly clarify this scaling effect and include 
references demonstrating kinetic separatrix features in the solar wind. In particular, we 
have added the following paragraphs:  
“The apparent morphological differences between the boundaries in the solar wind and 
the magnetosphere can be largely attributed to the vast difference in system sizes. In the 
heliospheric current sheet, the exhaust width can be extremely large ($\sim 4000~d_i$). 
Consequently, an EBCS layer of $\sim 20~d_i$ thickness appears as a sharp, step-like 
discontinuity on macroscopic scales, leading to its historical interpretation as an MHD 
slow-mode shock. In contrast, in near-Earth environments where the entire exhaust 
width may only be tens of $d_i$, the EBCS occupies a much larger relative fraction of 
the system, making gradients appear less abrupt. Furthermore, a recent observation 
shows that the localized electron diffusion region in solar wind reconnection can be 
bursty and turbulent, the resulting macroscopic exhaust boundaries can still propagate 
as highly coherent, long-lived structures \citep{Wang2023}. 
Additionally, while the limited temporal resolution of interplanetary spacecraft has 
historically obscured micro-physical features like plasma waves and parallel electric 
fields at solar wind separatrices, high-resolution measurements have confirmed their 
presence. This includes observations of energetic proton beams on HCS separatrix field 
lines \citep{Phan2022} and electrostatic waves localized at solar wind current sheets 

=== PAGE 7 ===
\citep{Malaspina2013}. Therefore, rather than categorizing solar wind exhausts 
exclusively as macroscopic fluid shocks, our results suggest that the boundaries of 
prolonged reconnection across all these environments can be explained by the two-fluid 
kinetic framework of EBCS.” 
2.​ Another main issue is the claim of quasi-steady reconnection. In the magnetosphere, the 
reconnection both at the magnetopause and in the magnetotail are bursty rather than 
quasi-steady reconnection, even it can persistent for hours. In PIC simulations, the 
reconnection rate is generally temporal variation and cannot be steady also. In the solar 
wind, the ion jets or exhausts are generally called quasi-steady reconnection, since the 
similar flows were repeatedly observed by different spacecrafts during hours (e.g., Phan 
et al., nature 2006). However, this explanation is not unique. The steady ion jets do NOT 
mean the corresponding reconnection is steady also. The reconnection could be still 
bursty and the resulting outflow jets keep steady during their propagation in the 
interplanetary space. Recently, based on an MMS observation in the solar wind, it shows 
that the reconnection responsible for the steady outflows is bursty and turbulent (e.g., 
Wang et al., Nature Astronomy 2023). So, this reviewer suggests avoid the steady 
reconnection in the paper, because the reconnection in the magnetosphere and 
laboratory must be bursty. ​
​
Regarding the excellent recent work by Wang et al. (2023), we agree that the 
microscopic dynamics at the reconnection X-line (the electron diffusion region) are 
frequently bursty, temporally varying, and turbulent across the magnetosphere, 
laboratory plasmas, and the solar wind. 
We agree with the reviewer that our use of the term 'quasi-steady reconnection' required 
a more precise definition. By 'quasi-steady,' we refer to a system where the macroscopic 
global reconnection rate (e.g., Comisso and Bhattacharjee 2016) remains steady over a 
prolonged period. While the instantaneous electric field at the local X-line can be highly 
dynamic or bursty (even in our controlled MRX experiments) the time-averaged global 
reconnection rate is continuous. It is this global steadiness that allows macroscopic 
boundary structures like the EBCS to persist. We have clarified this critical distinction 
between local X-line burstiness and global quasi-steadiness in the revised text. 
Crucially, the Wang et al. (2023) paper perfectly encapsulates the very physical picture 
we are conveying. While they observe a highly turbulent diffusion region, they explicitly 
note that "Far away from the turbulent reconnection site, a Petschek-type exhaust 
bounded by the slow-mode shock was detected." This observation perfectly aligns with 
and supports our conclusion: even if the localized X-line is bursty, the boundaries 
organizing the extended exhaust (which fluid models interpret as slow-mode shocks and 
our kinetic framework identifies as EBCS) act as robust, macroscopically stable 
structures. 
Regarding the rarity of EBCS in the magnetosphere compared to the solar wind: As 
noted in our original manuscript, we attribute this to the stability of global boundary

conditions and the plasma β. The global conditions of the magnetosphere change much 
more rapidly than those of the heliospheric current sheet (HCS), making it much rarer to 
achieve and maintain the prolonged quasi-steady conditions required for EBCS. 
Furthermore, in low-β regimes typical of many magnetotail events, the current sheet is 
highly susceptible to fragmentation via plasmoid formation or the lower hybrid drift 
instability (LHDI), which destroys the EBCS structure. We have revised the Introduction 
and Discussion sections to make these definitions and environmental distinctions much 
clearer.​

1) Difference in Bz profiles:

5) Rankine-Hugoniot (RH) relations for the solar wind event:​
We sincerely thank the reviewer for this suggestion. Prompted by your comment, we 
evaluated the macroscopic 1D MHD jump conditions across the sharp boundaries in the 

=== PAGE 12 ===
PSP event. We found that strict 1D Rankine-Hugoniot (RH) relations (such as 1D mass 
flux conservation and static pressure balance) are not perfectly satisfied across these 
specific boundaries. 
However, this discrepancy is not a violation of fundamental conservation laws, but rather 
a reflection of the inherently two-dimensional, dynamic nature of the reconnection 
exhaust. The apparent failure of 1D mass conservation (where density increases without 
a proportional drop in normal velocity) is naturally explained by 2D mass injection from 
the X-line flowing along the separatrix. Similarly, static macroscopic force imbalances are 
accounted for by active plasma acceleration and deceleration within the exhaust. 
Because these 2D dynamic effects make it difficult to accurately treat the boundaries as 
strictly 1D macroscopic slow-mode shocks, our study focuses specifically on the 
localized kinetic mechanisms (i.e., EBCS) that dictate the structure of the transition layer 
itself. We have added text to the solar wind section to explicitly clarify this physical 
context and why evaluating these boundaries as strictly 1D fluid discontinuities is 
insufficient.

In this paper, the authors investigated the reconnection separatrix region in different plasma 
environments, including magnetosphere, solar wind, and laboratory experiment, via spacecraft 
observations, laboratory experiment and PIC simulations. They concluded that the expanding bifurcated 
current sheets (EBCS) are universal structure for quasi-steady reconnection regardless of the system 
size. The separatrix region is indeed an important region during magnetic reconnection, both in the 
planet's magnetosphere and solar wind, and shows very dynamical micro-physics. However, the features 
along separatrix region during reconnection in solar wind and in the magnetosphere are dramatically 
different. In the magnetosphere, enhanced plasma waves, especially electrostatic waves, strong parallel 
electric field structure, plasma density peak/cavity, temperature decreases, intense electron beam 
parallel to the magnetic field, and strong parallel current are generally observed. Of course, the sub-
alfvenic ion jets are bounded by the separatrix regions. Some of these features are consistent with EBCS 
in this paper. On the other hand, in the solar wind, a pair of the slow-mode shocks or discontinuities are 
detected in the boundary of reconnection-related sub-alfvenic ion jets which has been thought to be 
quasi-steady reconnection. In this paper, the separatrix region are called EBCS. However, the features 
along the EBCS in the solar wind are quite different from those in the magnetosphere. For example, in 
the solar wind, the sharp magnetic field gradient regarded as current layer was detected, without 
enhanced waves, electron beams etc. So, this reviewer does not think the authors have found many 
common features along the boundary of the reconnection outflow jets.   
 
Another main issue is the claim of quasi-steady reconnection. In the magnetosphere, the reconnection 
both at the magnetopause and in the magnetotail are bursty rather than quasi-steady reconnection, even 
it can persistent for hours. In PIC simulations, the reconnection rate is generally temporal variation and 
cannot be steady also. In the solar wind, the ion jets or exhausts are generally called quasi-steady 
reconnection, since the similar flows were repeatedly observed by different spacecrafts during hours 
(e.g., Phan et al., nature 2006). However, this explanation is not unique. The steady ion jets do NOT 
mean the corresponding reconnection is steady also. The reconnection could be still bursty and the 
resulting outflow jets keep steady during their propagation in the interplanetary space. Recently, based 
on an MMS observation in the solar wind, it shows that the reconnection responsible for the steady 
outflows is bursty and turbulent (e.g., Wang et al., Nature Astronomy 2023). So, this reviewer suggests 
avoid the steady reconnection in the paper, because the reconnection in the magnetosphere and 
laboratory must be bursty.

length scale of the potential drop. This is important because the existence of a large potential drop
alone does not explain the localized nature of the current sheet and the electric field. The localized
current on each side of the bifurcation signifies a localized Hall field, which means the potential
drop must happen over a short distance. Why it occurs over such short distance is not addressed
by Eq. 5 and its derivation. If the same potential drop occurs over a much larger spatial distance,

address the origin of EBCS.

are the species that control the EM field (e.g., Eq. 1) are all over the place. There isn’t a single

cause of the EBCS through these simulations. Also, the plasma beta for the simulation in Figure
2 is not even mentioned, and from Reference 27, we only guess it to be 0.03. This is similar to the
simulation in Figure 8, which the authors show as an example of absence of EBCS (this point is
also related to point 1 above).
