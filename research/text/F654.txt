Duskward displacement of plasmoids 
and reconnection in the near‑Earth magnetotail
Akimasa Ieda1*    and Yukinaga Miyashita2,3 
Abstract 
Magnetic reconnection in the near-Earth magnetotail is responsible for explosive release of energy during substorms 
and auroral breakups. This near-tail reconnection was previously assumed to occur around the midnight meridian, 
where earthward flows were typically observed. Based on observations of tailward-moving plasmoids, the Geotail spacecraft 
mission discovered that the reconnection location was displaced toward dusk. This dusk preference is presumably caused 
by the Hall electric field, as was suggested later in simulations. However, recent spacecraft observations have indicated 
that the reconnection was displaced toward dawn, and not dusk, in Mercury’s magnetotail. In response to this controversy, 
our study aims to clarify the dawn–dusk location of fast plasma flows in the near-Earth magnetotail. Through a comprehen-
sive reinterpretation and integration of previous statistical results, we found that the dusk preference is generally evident 
for tailward flows but is often absent for earthward flows. These results indicate that the statistical results of earthward flows 
are sensitive to event selection criteria. We conclude that the dawn–dusk location of earthward flow is statistically unclear 
at the time of substorm onset. Similarly, in the magnetotail of other planets, the dawn–dusk location of planetward flow may 
be sensitive to event selection criteria. Hence, reconnection may occur predominantly on the duskside in Mercury’s magne-
totail. This hypothesis will be tested using observations of tailward-moving plasmoids by the BepiColombo Mio spacecraft, 
which will begin orbital observations of Mercury in the year 2026.

1  Introduction
Magnetic reconnection occurs at two locations in the 
magnetotail of the Earth. One reconnection site is located 
approximately 20 ­RE down the tail (Ieda et  al. 1998; 
Miyashita et al. 1999) and is called the near-Earth neutral 
line (NENL). The other site is located approximately 100 
­RE down the tail (Slavin et al. 1985; Nishida et al. 1996) 
and is called the distant neutral line (DNL). In this study, 
we focus on the dawn–dusk location of the NENL.
The NENL model is the most popular framework for 
understanding substorms (Coppi et  al. 1966; Atkinson 
1966; Hones et  al. 1973; Nishida and Nagayama 1973; 
Russell and McPherron 1973; Hones 1976; Baker et  al. 
1996; Shiokawa et al. 1998; Sergeev et al. 2012; Ieda et al. 
2016). This model assumes that the NENL formation 
causes substorms, which are explosive energy releases 
and optically manifest as auroral breakups (e.g., Akasofu 
1963; Ieda et al. 2018). Temporal and spatial associations 
between the NENL and substorm onsets have been stud-
ied to evaluate the NENL model of substorms.
Direct identification of NENL via in situ satellite obser-
vations is quite rare, particularly near the time of sub-
storm onset, primarily due to its small size. Instead, the 
NENL is often identified indirectly by fast plasma flow, 
which is remote  evidence of reconnection (Fig.  1). The 
NENL presumably creates a pair of fast plasma flows: 
an earthward flow with a northward magnetic field and 
a tailward flow with a southward magnetic field. Accord-
ing to the NENL model, this flow pattern should form at 
the time of substorm onset, as supported in case studies 
using two-satellite observations (Petrukovich et al. 1998; 
Petrukovich and Yahnin 2006; Slavin et al. 2002).
Prior to the Geotail satellite mission, the NENL was 
implicitly recognized to occur around the midnight 
meridian. This midnight preference of NENL was consist-
ent with observations of earthward flows: Baumjohann 
et  al. (1990) and Angelopoulos et  al. (1994) observed 
that earthward flows are centered around midnight, as 
detailed in Sect. 3.1.
In magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations, the 
NENL was located near midnight. Hesse and Birn (1991) 
assumed that the plasma sheet was the thinnest near 
midnight under the initial conditions of their MHD sim-
ulation. The resultant earthward flow was the “fastest 
in the midnight region” (p. 19,418). Walker et al. (1993) 
reported that the reconnection first occurred near mid-
night in their MHD simulation. They interpreted that this 
midnight preference was caused by the northward com-
ponent of the magnetic field being “smaller in the central 
part of the tail than near the magnetopause” (p. 17,241).
The Geotail spacecraft mission (Nishida 1994) discov-
ered that the NENL is displaced toward dusk. This dusk 
preference was reported for the first time in two studies: 
Ieda et  al. (1998) with tailward-moving plasmoids and 
Nagai et al. (1998) with fast flows. Later, this dusk prefer-
ence was suggested to be caused by the Hall effect in sim-
ulations (Lin et al. 2014; Lu et al. 2018). The Hall electric 
field toward the north–south center is formed in the thin 
current sheet and transports the magnetic flux dawn-
ward. Accordingly, the duskside plasma sheet is evacu-
ated and further thins, leading to reconnection.
However, the MErcury Surface, Space ENvironment, 
GEochemistry, and Ranging (MESSENGER) spacecraft 
observed that the reconnection location was displaced 
toward dawn, not dusk, in the magnetotail of Mercury 
(Sun et al. 2016; Smith et al. 2017). The cause of the dif-
ference in the reconnection dawn–dusk location between 
Earth and Mercury remains unclear.
This report aims to clarify the dawn–dusk location of 
fast flows in the near-Earth magnetotail by elaborating 
and unifying previous observations. We found that dusk 
preference is a robust feature for tailward flows, but is 
variable among studies of earthward flows. For example, 
in Nagai et al. (1998), earthward flows were not displaced 
toward dusk but toward dawn.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. 
Section 2 reviews the discovery of dusk preference. Sec-
tion 3 clarifies that the dawn–dusk location of earthward 
flow is variable among studies. Section 4 clarifies that no 
statistical studies have demonstrated the dusk preference 
of earthward flows within 10 min of substorm onset. Sec-
tion 5 discusses why statistical results of earthward flows 
are sensitive to event selection criteria. Section  6 con-
cludes that the dawn–dusk locations of earthward flows 
are unknown at the times of substorm onsets. Appendix 
A explains the aberrated geocentric solar magnetospheric 
(AGSM) coordinate system, which is more appropriate 
than the geocentric solar magnetospheric (GSM) coor-
dinate system for studying the magnetotail. Particularly, 
AGSM should be adopted for studying asymmetries rela-
tive to the tail axis.

2  Discovery of dusk preference of reconnection 
in the near‑Earth magnetotail
The NENL is widely recognized as displaced toward 
dusk (e.g., Walsh et  al. 2014; Sitnov et  al. 2019; Chen 
et  al. 2019; Sun et  al. 2022). This dusk preference was 
first reported by Ieda et al. (1998) and Nagai et al. (1998). 
Earthward flows were displaced toward dawn in Nagai 
et al. (1998).
2.1  Tailward‑moving plasmoids by Ieda et al. (1998)
Ieda et al. (1998) found that tailward-moving plasmoids 
tended to be observed around YAGSM = 3 ­RE in the dusk 
sector (Fig. 2). They concluded that “The center of plas-
moids in the near tail, and possibly that of the NENL, are 
likely to be located around YGSM′ = 3 RE ” (p. 4,463).
Detailed explanations of their analysis methods are 
provided below. Ieda et  al. (1998) used AGSM coordi-
nates (see Appendix A). They identified tailward-moving 
plasmoids in the magnetotail using Geotail observations 
of the magnetic field and thermal ions. The number of 
observed plasmoids in each XY grid was normalized to 
the length of time of Geotail observations within 10 
­RE of a model neutral sheet. The resultant occurrence 
rates between 25 and 50 ­RE down the tail are shown in 
Fig. 2b. They fit the calculated occurrence rates using a 
Gaussian function. The fitted occurrence rate peaked at 
YAGSM = 3.2 ­RE on the duskside.
2.2  Fast flows within 10 min of substorm onset  
by Nagai et al. (1998)
Nagai et al. (1998) calculated the occurrence rate of fast 
flows. Tailward flows tended to be observed in the dusk. 
Although earthward flows tended to be observed in the 
dawn in their figures, they did not claim this feature. 
Instead, they recognized that merged “tailward or earth-
ward flows” (p. 4,424) were skewed toward dusk. They 
concluded that “Magnetic reconnection usually starts in 
the premidnight sector of the magnetotail” (p. 4,419).
Nagai et al. (1998) used AGSM coordinates. They first 
identified the timing of the substorm onsets using the Pi2 
pulsation in the ground magnetic field. They then studied 
the magnetic field and thermal ions observed by Geotail 
within 10  min of the onsets. They classified the events 
into four types: (a) tailward flow events (Fig.  3a) if the 
20-min interval included at least one data sample with 
the X (sunward) component of the plasma velocity, Vx, ≤ 
−300 km/s: (b) similarly, earthward flow events (Fig. 3b) 
if Vx ≥ 300 km/s; the remaining events classified using 
the ion plasma beta βi as (c) steady-state plasma sheet 
events if βi ≥ 0.1; (d) lobe events if βi < 0.1.
The occurrence rates of the tailward flows were calcu-
lated as the number of (a) tailward flow events divided by 
the number of [(a) + (b) + (c)] events. The occurrence rates 
of the (b) earthward flows were calculated similarly. These 
occurrence rates were calculated for each of the 5-RE bins

2.3  Implication of “tailward or earthward flows”  
in Nagai et al. (1998)
Nagai et  al. (1998) concluded a dusk preference of the 
NENL despite the dawn preference of earthward flows in 
their figures. In this section, we discuss the logic behind 
this conclusion.
The occurrence rate was much higher for tailward flows 
than for earthward flows (Fig. 3c). Accordingly, “tailward 
or earthward flows” (Sect. 2.2) in the −30 < XAGSM < −10 
­RE range practically corresponded to tailward flows in 
their particular data set.
However, the occurrence rate is biased by the satellite X 
locations. For example, if a satellite is more often located 
close to Earth (i.e., inside XAGSM  ~ −20 ­RE), the occur-
rence rate of earthward flows should be much higher 
than that of tailward flows. In such cases, “tailward or 
earthward flows” correspond to earthward flows.
Hence, tailward and earthward flows should be con-
sidered separately when their predominant dawn–dusk 
locations are distinct. The dawn preference of earth-
ward flows in Fig. 3f of Nagai et al. (1998) (i.e., Fig. 3c 
of the present study) conventionally indicates that mag-
netic reconnection usually occurs on the dawnside, not 
duskside. This contradicts their conclusion.

3  Diverging results on the dawn–dusk location 
of earthward flows
Earthward flows were observed on the dawnside by 
Nagai et al. (1998) as described in Sect. 2.2. However, the 
dawn–dusk location of earthward flows is variable among 
studies. It can be at either dawn, midnight, or dusk, as 
reviewed in this section and summarized in Table  1. 
These discordant findings suggest that earthward flows 
are sensitive to the selection criteria.
3.1  Midnight‑preferred earthward flows
Imber et al. (2011) statistically studied flux ropes using 
THEMIS satellite observations of magnetic fields. Tail-
ward-moving structures were observed on the dusk-
side (Fig.  4). In contrast, the  dusk preference was not 
observed for earthward-moving structures inside X = −18 
­RE, as they concluded that “our distribution of Earthward 
events becomes more symmetric within −18 ­RE” (p. 11).
Angelopoulos et  al. (1994) statistically studied earth-
ward flows using AMPTE/IRM and ISEE-2 satellite 
observations of magnetic fields and thermal ions. They 
recognized that earthward flows were centered near mid-
night, as follows. Earthward flows were required to have a 
peak velocity exceeding 400 km/s. The occurrence rates of 
earthward flows are shown in Fig. 5. Skews may be visible 
to dusk (YAGSM ~ 1 ­RE) in the IRM and dawn (YAGSM ~ −4 
­RE) in ISEE-2. However, these skews were not mentioned 
by Angelopoulos et  al. (1994), presumably because they 
were not considered statistically significant. Instead, they 
stated that “The spatial dependence of the earthward BBF 
occurrence rate on both satellites is roughly similar to the 
one of high speed flows in the plasma sheet described by 
Baumjohann et al. (1990) for the IRM data set, i.e., it tends 
to increase with distance from Earth and proximity to 
midnight.” (p. 21,267). For clarification, Imber et al. (2011) 
likely misrecognized in their Table 1 that earthward flows

Nakamura et  al. (2001b) statistically studied earth-
ward flows using Geotail satellite observations of mag-
netic fields and thermal ions. These earthward flows did 
not have a dusk preference and were located near mid-
night, as follows. Figure 6 shows the magnetic local time 
(MLT) distribution of fast earthward flows. The front-side 
boundary of these earthward flows was assumed to be 
curved rather than straight. Accordingly, the azimuthal 
angle of earthward flow is antisymmetric between the 
dawn and dusk sides of the center of earthward flows. 
They stated that “In a statistical sense this change in the 
angles about the midnight region suggests that the flow is 
confined in the midnight region” (p. 10,798). 
Juusola et  al. (2011) suggested a dusk preference for 
earthward flows using data from Geotail, Cluster, and 
THEMIS satellites. They claimed that “during the sub-
storm expansion phase, the average speed was more 
enhanced on the duskside than dawnside” (paragraph 
39). Although they did not explicitly specify, this state-
ment most likely refers to their Fig. 5d, where the average 
speed of earthward flows is shown by the colors on the 
XY plane. Although the dusk preference may slightly exist 
beyond 20 ­RE down the tail, it does not appear to exist 
inside 20 ­RE, where earthward flows appear symmetric 
around midnight. Thus, their dusk preference is not likely 
associated with the NENL at the time of substorm onset 
(i.e., substorm expansion phase onset).
3.2  Dusk‑preferred earthward flows
Some studies have reported a dusk preference for earth-
ward flows. However, the dusk preference of earthward 
flows has not yet been statistically demonstrated within 
10 min of the substorm onset.
Nagai and Machida (1998) observed that earthward 
flows were displaced toward dusk during the substorm 
expansion phase. Such earthward flows were likely 
observed more than 10  min after substorm onset, as 
detailed in Sect. 4.4.

Raj et  al. (2002) suggested a dusk preference for fast 
flows. They identified flows faster than 250 km/s as meas-
ured by the Wind spacecraft inside 25 ­RE down the tail, 
regardless of geomagnetic conditions. They stated that 
“Essentially all bulk flows are earthward directed.” (para-
graph 34). They did not state the numerical value of this 
duskward displacement. The bottom right panel of their 
Fig.  12 shows the occurrence rate of fast flows against 
YGSM. The maximum occurrence rate was located at 
YGSM = 14 ­RE.
McPherron et  al. (2011) concluded a dusk prefer-
ence for earthward flows near XGSM = −10 ­RE using 
THEMIS D spacecraft data. Geomagnetic conditions do 
not appear to have been considered for this conclusion. 
They identified earthward flows by a peak X component 
of the perpendicular velocity exceeding 150  km/s. This 
relatively slow speed was adopted as a criterion presum-
ably because “the earthward flow speed decreases inside 
of 12 ­RE” (paragraph 32). They nominally assumed that 
these slow “fast flows” were “produced by reconnection” 
(paragraph 56). They claimed that the local time distribu-
tion of their earthward flows is virtually identical to that 
of auroral expansion onsets reported by Frey et al. (2004).
Li et al. (2021) appear to have updated the analysis of 
McPherron et al. (2011) by reporting a dusk preference 
for earthward flows near XGSM = −10 ­RE using THEMIS 
A, D, and E spacecraft data. They identified earthward 
flows by a peak XY component of the perpendicular 
velocity exceeding 150  km/s. Substorm-onset intervals 
were identified as within 15  min of the onset of mag-
netic positive bays on the ground. They observed dusk 
preferences during the substorm-onset and non-sub-
storm intervals. We recognize the maximum occurrence 
rate of earthward flows at 22 MLT and YGSM ~ 4 ­RE (i.e., 
YAGSM ~ 3 ­RE) in their Fig.  5. Their earthward flows are 
not traditional “fast” earthward flows, due to the slow 
150  km/s criterion. Their earthward flows have likely 
been slowed and deflected due to interactions with the 
inner magnetosphere; thus, their MLT is not directly 
applicable to infer the MLT of NENL.
For clarification, more earthward flows were observed 
on the duskside than on the dawnside in Fig. 2 of Naka-
mura et al. (1991) and Fig. 3a of Slavin et al. (2005); how-
ever, the corresponding occurrence rate normalized by 
length of observation time was not shown; accordingly, 
no dusk preference was concluded in these studies.
4  Timing of earthward flows
Earthward flows are rarely observed within a few minutes 
of substorm onset. Instead, earthward flows are typically 
observed later, particularly at the time of tailward retreat 
of the NENL at the beginning of the substorm recovery 
phase. No statistical study has demonstrated the dusk-
ward displacement of earthward flow within 10  min of 
substorm onset.
4.1  Rarity of earthward flows at substorm onsets
Earthward flows are rarely identified in statistical stud-
ies around the time of substorm onset. Lui et al. (1998) 
showed plasma velocity vectors in the XY plane observed 
by Geotail for 102 substorm events in their Fig.  3. 
Machida et  al. (1999) showed similar velocity vectors 
for 263 substorm events in their Fig. 1. Although neither 
study provided occurrence rates, earthward flows were 
much rarer than tailward flows.
Miyashita et  al. (2009) showed the average flows 
around the time of substorm onset in their Fig. 2. They 
observed that “fast tailward flows begin to grow signifi-
cantly in the premidnight sector at X < −20 ­RE at onset 
(t = 0)” (paragraph 17). In contrast, they did not claim a 
premidnight preference of earthward flows. We recog-
nize in their Fig.  2 that the dawn–dusk location of the 
earthward flows appeared to change randomly with time 
and did not align with the longitude of the tailward flows. 
This random location of earthward flows is presumably 
due to their rarity.
4.2  Later earthward flows and tailward retreat 
of the reconnection site
Earthward flows tend to be observed much later than 
the onset of substorms. Nakamura et al. (2001a) summa-
rized in their Table 1 that earthward flows were typically 
observed during the substorm recovery phase. Baumjo-
hann et al. (1991) and Juusola et al. (2011) showed that 
earthward flows reach the maximum occurrence rate at 
the beginning of the substorm recovery phase, which is 
conventionally identified by the recovery of magnetic 
negative bays at auroral latitudes on the ground (e.g., 
Hones et al. 1973).
Earthward flows observed at the beginning of the sub-
storm recovery phase correspond to the tailward retreat 
of the NENL. The NENL does not move significantly dur-
ing the substorm expansion phase (Nishida and Nagay-
ama 1973). Later, it suddenly retreats tailward at the 
beginning of the substorm recovery phase (Hones et al. 
1973; Baumjohann et al. 1999). Ieda et al. (2016) found 
that this tailward retreat occurs stepwise in multiple-
onset substorms. Hence, tailward retreat and associated 
earthward flows sometimes also occur during the middle 
expansion phase.

4.3  Direct observation of reconnection site 
at the beginning of the substorm recovery phase
The NENL is small and, thus, usually difficult to observe 
directly (i.e., locally) by satellite. The NENL is directly 
observed only when it moves, and thus is typically 
observed at the time of its tailward retreat at the begin-
ning of the substorm recovery phase. When the NENL 
passes tailward over a satellite, the satellite observes a 
flow reversal: from a tailward flow with southward mag-
netic field to an earthward flow with northward magnetic 
field. The directly observed NENL is displaced toward 
dusk (Ueno et al. 1999; Nagai et al. 2015).
Ueno et al. (1999) identified 5940 passages of the NENL 
over the Geotail spacecraft by the flow and magnetic-
field reversal. For the first time, they found that directly 
observed NENLs were statistically displaced toward 
dusk. The NENL was often observed in the region of  
5 < YGSM <15 ­RE near XGSM = −28 ­RE. We estimate that 
this dusk preference should be valid under AGSM coor-
dinates (i.e.,  3 < YAGSM < 13 ­RE, i.e., YAGSM ~ 8 ­RE), as the 
aberration effect is approximately 2 ­RE.
Nagai et al. (2015) confirmed the dusk preference using 
a refined direct identification of 71 NENL events. In addi-
tion to the flow and magnetic-field reversal, Geotail was 
required to observe strong electron heating, ensuring that 
the satellite entered the ion–electron decoupling region. 
They estimated that the NENL was typically located 
at  3 < YGSM < 9 ­RE. We estimate that this corresponds to 
approximately 1 < YAGSM < 7 ­RE, i.e., YAGSM ~ 4 ­RE.
The typical location of NENL at the beginning of the 
substorm recovery phase (YAGAM ~ 4 ­RE in Nagai et  al. 
(2015)) is close to that at substorm onset (YAGSM ~ 3 ­RE 
in Ieda et al. (2008)). This closeness is in part a natural 
consequence as the directly observed events are a subset 
of tailward flow events. However, it may also be due to 
a coincidence caused by unknown biases in the selection 
of the directly observed rare events because the YAGAM 
location of the NENL may change with time from the fol-
lowing points of view. One perspective is that the NENL 
may extend in the dawn or dusk directions in simulations 
(Shay et al. 2003; Nakamura et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2019). 
Additionally, the brightest part of auroral breakup tends 
to move westward (i.e., the westward traveling surge) 
(e.g., Akasofu 1963, 1964; Rothwell et  al. 1984; Lyons 
et al. 2013; Ebihara and Tanaka 2015).
For clarification, we recognize that the dusk prefer-
ence was not necessarily statistically significant in the 
following two studies. Rogers et al. (2019) identified 12 
NENL events using data from the magnetospheric mul-
tiscale (MMS) spacecraft. Eleven of 12 events were on 
the duskside in GSM coordinates. However, based on 
the YGSM of the NENL in their Table 2, we predict that 
approximately nine of 12 events were on the duskside in 
AGSM coordinates.
Eastwood et  al. (2010) identified NENL events using 
data from the Cluster spacecraft and showed the loca-
tions of 18 identified events in GSM coordinates in their 
Fig.  4. They stated that the NENL was observed “more 
often on the premidnight side of the magnetotail. How-
ever, these data are a strong function of the Cluster 
orbit, and so one should be careful when drawing con-
clusions about the most typical location of the diffu-
sion region” (paragraph 22). We recognize that by “more 
often” they intend to state that there were more events 
on the premidnight side in their Fig. 4. That is, they did 
not mention the corresponding occurrence rate normal-
ized by length of observation time; accordingly, they do 
not presumably intend to, and indeed cannot, conclude 
the dusk preference of the NENL.
4.4  Earthward flows during substorm expansion phase 
by Nagai and Machida (1998)
Earthward flows are displaced toward dawn in Nagai 
et al. (1998). In contrast, they are displaced toward dusk 
in Nagai and Machida (1998) (hereinafter NM1998). In 
this section, we discuss that this dusk preference was 
caused by adding an extra interval beginning 10 min after 
substorm onset.
NM1998 is an article in a monograph and primarily 
reviews past studies, including Nagai et al. (1998). How-
ever, regarding the dawn–dusk location of the recon-
nection site, NM1998 conducted a new analysis in their 
Sect. 3.1 similar to that of Nagai et al. (1998). The selec-
tion criteria for the target interval and fast flow differ 
between the two studies. NM1998 did not discuss the dif-
ferences in criteria and results between the two studies. 
Considering that NM1998 re-analyzed data and obtained 
the opposite result on the dawn–dusk location, they pre-
sumably recognized that the previous analysis of Nagai 
et al. (1998) must be revisited.
Earthward flows appear to have a dusk preference in 
NM1998, as shown in Fig. 7. The dusk preference is based 
on eight earthward flows in the dusk between 7 and 29 
­RE down the tail, as we indicated by the green rectan-
gle in Fig. 7b. However, we cannot identify correspond-
ing earthward flows in Nagai et al. (1998), as shown in 
Fig. 3b, except for the two events indicated by the red cir-
cles in Fig. 7b.
The criteria for the target interval were revised as fol-
lows. Nagai et  al. (1998) studied the interval within 
10 min of substorm onset (i.e., −10 < T < 10 min, where 
T = 0 refers to substorm onsets), which they identified 
based on the Pi2 onset in the ground magnetic field. In 
contrast, NM1998 studied the interval of the substorm 

Page 9 of 17
Ieda and Miyashita ﻿Earth, Planets and Space  (2024) 76:159
	
expansion phase, which they identified as the interval 
between the start and peak of a mid-latitude positive bay 
in the ground magnetic field (p. 212). Although the dura-
tion of the substorm expansion phase is not mentioned 
in NM1998, it is generally 20–45 min (e.g., McPherron 
and Chu 2018; Ieda et al. 2008; Baumjohann et al. 1991, 
1999). Accordingly, NM1998 studied intervals of approx-
imately 0 < T < 20–45  min. For clarification, the interval 
before substorm onset was excluded from NM1998 for 
unknown reasons, although this interval was emphasized 
by Nagai et al. (1998). In summary, an interval that starts 
at T = 10 min was not included in Nagai et al. (1998), but 
was added in NM1998.
The criterion for earthward flow was Vx ≥ 300  km/s 
in Nagai et al. (1998), as explained in Sect. 2.2. In con-
trast, NM1998 required a northward magnetic field and 
Vperp,x ≥ 300 km/s, where a velocity perpendicular to the 
magnetic field was considered. The criteria for tailward 
flows were similarly updated.
Although the criteria for earthward flow are less 
restrictive in Nagai et al. (1998) than in NM1998, the six 
earthward flows are not included in Nagai et al. (1998) 
but are included in NM1998. Thus, the six earthward 
flows are likely identified during the extra interval after 
T = 10 min in NM1998.
In summary, we infer that most (i.e., six of eight) earth-
ward flows on the duskside in Fig.  7b (i.e., Fig.  2b of 
NM1998) were observed more than 10  min after sub-
storm onset. Only two earthward flows were observed on 
the duskside before T =10 min in NM1998. Furthermore, 
only one earthward flow event was observed on the dusk-
side between 13 and 28 ­RE down the tail in NM1998. 
Thus, the dusk preference of earthward flow is not dem-
onstrated at the time of substorm onset in NM1998. In 
fact, Nagai et al. (1998) and NM1998 made no statement 
on statistical dawn–dusk locations of earthward flows in 
their text.

5  Discussion
Sections 5.1–5.3 discuss why the dawn–dusk location of 
earthward flows is sensitive to event selection criteria. 
Sections 5.4–5.5 confirm that plasma injection, magnetic 
dipolarization, and auroral breakup are displaced toward 
dusk. These phenomena can be located duskside of the 
NENL in terms of the substorm current wedge and by the 
possible deflection of earthward flows near XAGSM =  − 10 
­RE. Section  5.6 offers possible explanations for the 
dawn–dusk location of the reconnection site that differs 
between Earth and Mercury.
5.1  Paired earthward and tailward flows 
not yet demonstrated
As mentioned in Sect. 1, the NENL model of substorms 
requires a pair of earthward and tailward flows at the 
time of substorm onset. In this model, the dawn–dusk 
location of the two flows is implicitly assumed to be the 
same (Fig.  1). Although this assumption is presumably 
correct, this feature has not yet been statistically dem-
onstrated for the time of substorm onset, possibly due to 
observational biases, as follows.
In Sect.  2, tailward flows were discovered to be dis-
placed toward dusk. This preference has been robustly 
observed (e.g., Ieda et al. 1998, 2008; Nagai et al. 1998; 
Machida et al. 1999; Miyashita et al. 2009; Imber et al. 
2011). Accordingly, the NENL forms on the duskside.
In contrast, the dawn–dusk location of earthward flows 
varies among studies. It can be either dawn, midnight, 
or dusk, as described in Sect. 3. In particular, no statis-
tical study has demonstrated the duskward displacement 
of earthward flows within 10 min of substorm onset, as 
discussed in Sects. 3.2 and 4. These discordant findings 
indicate that the statistical results of earthward flows are 
sensitive to event selection criteria.
This sensitivity is presumably due to observational 
biases in earthward flows. One potential bias is caused 
by the fact that earthward flows do not necessarily origi-
nate from the NENL, as detailed in Sect.  5.2. Another 
potential bias is caused by the variability in the thick-
ness in ZAGSM of earthward flows (Sect. 5.3). In addition, 
earthward flows are narrow and typically 2 ­RE in YAGSM in 
multi-spacecraft observations (Sergeev et al. 1996; Naka-
mura et al. 2004), causing them to be missed, depending 
on selection criteria. They are much narrower than tail-
ward flows, which are typically 10 ­RE in YAGSM or 2 h in 
MLT at XAGSM ~ −28 ­RE (Ieda et al. 2008).
Nagai et  al. (1998) found that earthward flows were 
displaced toward dawn. Although this was not reflected 
in NM1998 and is not widely appreciated in later stud-
ies, it can imply valuable information as a consequence 
of the adopted selection criteria. We propose one 
possible interpretation, involving a potential bias caused 
by a dawn–dusk asymmetry of the plasma sheet thick-
ness. The plasma sheet is  presumably thinner on the 
duskside than on the dawnside inside 20 ­RE down the tail 
before substorm onset, as can be inferred from observa-
tions of the northward magnetic field (Miyashita et  al. 
2009, paragraph 20) and current density (Petrukovich 
et al. 2009, paragraph 21). Accordingly, onset-associated 
earthward flows can be observed more readily on the 
dawnside or midnight even if they exist more often on 
the duskside.
For clarification, dawn–dusk asymmetries of the thin 
current sheet were reported by Rong et al. (2011), Arte-
myev et al. (2011), and Rogers et al. (2023). These stud-
ies do not mention the timing of the thin current sheet 
observation relative to substorm onset. The current sheet 
generally becomes very thin after the onset of reconnec-
tion and substorm, due to the evacuation of the plasma. 
Thus, the asymmetries were not necessarily observed 
before substorm onsets.
5.2  Tailward flows as strong evidence  
of near‑tail reconnection
Tailward flows provide strong evidence of reconnection. 
This is because tailward flows are typically accompanied 
by a southward magnetic field, which requires reconnec-
tion. In contrast, earthward flows provide weaker evi-
dence of reconnection as they are typically accompanied 
by a northward magnetic field, which originates from 
the Earth’s main field and thus does not require recon-
nection. For example, Lui (2009) reported that some 
earthward flows are caused by the near-Earth current dis-
ruption without tail reconnection.
Furthermore, tailward flows inside ~ 30 ­RE should 
originate from the NENL and not the DNL. The tailward 
flows typically occur within a few minutes of substorm 
onset (Moldwin and Hughes 1993; Nagai et  al. 1994; 
Ieda et  al. 2008). In contrast, earthward flows inside ~ 
30 ­RE do not necessarily originate from the NENL but 
can originate from the DNL, which is symmetric around 
YAGSM = 0 (Slavin et al. 1985). Earthward flows are rarely 
observed at the time of substorm onset (Sect. 4). Conse-
quently, tailward flows are better associated with global 
auroral activity compared to earthward flows are (Shue 
et  al. 2003, 2008; Ieda et  al. 2003, 2005) although both 
earthward flows (Fairfield et  al. 1999; Nakamura et  al. 
2001a) and tailward-moving plasmoids (Ieda et al. 2001) 
are consistently associated with auroral activity near the 
satellite footpoint.
The tailward flow from the NENL includes a magnetic 
loop called a plasmoid, which is observed by the space-
craft as a north-then-south variation in the magnetic 

Page 11 of 17
Ieda and Miyashita ﻿Earth, Planets and Space  (2024) 76:159
	
field. The northward magnetic field in the leading half of 
plasmoids is evidence that plasmoids originate from the 
NENL, not the DNL. Accordingly, plasmoids provide the 
strongest evidence of the NENL near substorm onset. 
However, any tailward flows well inside 100 ­RE down the 
tail are unlikely to originate from the DNL by definition. 
Such tailward flows with a southward magnetic field are 
practically equivalent to tailward-moving plasmoids in 
terms of the strong NENL evidence, in particular when 
tailward flows are relatively fast.
The dusk preference of the NENL was discovered by 
Geotail, which enabled the first statistical studies of tail-
ward flows. Geotail was the first satellite that intensively 
observed the equatorial magnetotail beyond 20 ­RE with 
appropriate thermal ion (Mukai et al. 1994) and magnetic 
field (Kokubun et al. 1994) instruments. The dusk prefer-
ence of the NENL was not recognized by previous satel-
lites like AMPTE/IRM and ISEE-2, presumably because 
tailward flows were rarely observed due to relatively low 
apogees inside approximately 20 ­RE. This indicates that 
the dawn–dusk location of NENL is difficult to conclude 
from statistical observations of earthward flows.
5.3  North–south thickness of flows
The dusk preference of tailward flows is robustly 
observed, presumably because tailward flows are thick 
in the ZAGSM direction. The YAGSM location of earthward 
flows varies among studies, presumably because earth-
ward flows can be thin or thick. We discuss the thickness 
of earthward and tailward flows in this section.
Statistical results of Asano et  al. (2004) suggest that 
earthward flows are thinner than tailward flows at the 
time of the substorm onset. They statistically studied the 
spatial distribution of the thin current sheet in the sub-
storm growth phase using Geotail data. They found that 
“the region where current sheet thinning occurs is located 
only on the Earthward side of the initial X line position. 
In other words, the initial X line forms near the tailward 
edge of the evolved thin current sheet.” (paragraph 14).
Earthward flows tend to be more often observed at 
XAGSM ~ −10 ­RE than at XAGSM ~ −16 ­RE around the times 
of substorm onset. A pair of earthward and tailward 
flows were observed in case studies using multi-satellite 
observations (Petrukovich et al. 1998; Slavin et al. 2002; 
Angelopoulos et  al. 2008). These earthward flows were 
observed at approximately −12 < XAGSM < −9 ­RE. A satel-
lite at XAGSM = −17 ­RE did not observe the corresponding 
earthward flow in the event reported by Angelopoulos 
et al. (2008).
These case studies suggest that earthward flows are 
thin at XAGSM ~ −16 ­RE and simultaneously thick at 
XAGSM ~ −10 ­RE around the times of substorm onset. 
Note, as a representative example, Asano et  al. (2003) 
conducted a case study of the thin current sheet in a sub-
storm observed by Geotail at XGSM = −15 ­RE. Earthward 
flows are opposed by the inner magnetosphere and slow 
down near the dipole-tail boundary around XAGSM = −10 
­RE. Earthward flows presumably thicken due to this inter-
action. Earthward flows are also observed at the tailward 
retreat (Sect. 4.2) at the beginning of the substorm recov-
ery phase, which is accompanied by the thickening of the 
plasma sheet (Hones et al. 1973).
5.4  Dusk preference of injection and dipolarization
Plasma injection and magnetic dipolarization are report-
edly observed to be displaced toward dusk between 
approximately 7–12 ­RE down the tail.
Arnoldy and Moore (1983) observed a dusk prefer-
ence for plasma injection and magnetic dipolarization 
in a geosynchronous orbit (6.6 ­RE). Using measurements 
from two GOES satellites, they studied the synchronous 
orbital manifestation of substorms, that is, plasma injec-
tion and dipolar magnetic field reconfiguration. They 
found that “the earliest onset sector at synchronous orbit 
is about 3  hour wide, skewed toward the evening side 
of midnight” (p. 6213). Nagai et  al. (1983) conducted a 
similar study and reached a similar conclusion that states 
“Substorm activity initially starts in a longitudinally lim-
ited sector that is situated in the premidnight region” (p. 
6,994).
Miyashita et  al. (2009) statistically studied magnetic 
dipolarization by calculating the time evolution of the 
average magnetic field. The dipolarization began around 
the time of the substorm onset at XGSM ~ −7 to −10 ­RE 
and YGSM ~ 4 ­RE, which corresponds to YAGSM ~ 3 ­RE 
when the aberration of ~ 0.7 ­RE is considered.
Liu et  al. (2013) statistically studied magnetic dipo-
larization, which they called “dipolarizing flux bundles.” 
They identified dipolarization events by rapid increases 
in the northward magnetic field. They did not study 
the timing of dipolarization relative to substorm onset. 
The occurrence rate of events was normalized by the 
THEMIS satellite orbit time in each bin. They showed 
that the resultant location of dipolarization was displaced 
toward dusk in GSM coordinates. Although they did 
not state the magnitude of this displacement, the occur-
rence rate appears the highest in the YGSM = 2–6 ­RE bin at 
XGSM = −10 ­RE in their Fig. 2c, thus at YAGSM ~ 1–5 ~ 3 ­RE.
5.5  Dusk preference of auroral breakup
Using global images onboard the Dynamics Explorer 
1 satellite, Craven and Frank (1991) found that auroral 

Page 12 of 17
Ieda and Miyashita ﻿Earth, Planets and Space  (2024) 76:159
breakups tend to occur on the duskside. They stated that 
“These observations confirm the well-known but not 
well-documented fact that auroral substorm onsets are 
observed most frequently late in the premidnight sector” 
(p. 284). They recognized that this MLT around 22.8 MLT 
is nearly identical to the 23 MLT of the plasma injection 
in Fig. 7 of Arnoldy and Moore (1983).
Corresponding fast flows and the NENL are suggested 
to be displaced to dawn from breakup MLTs. Nakamura 
et al. (2001b) found that earthward flows are displaced to 
dawn by 0.4 h in MLT from the center of auroral expan-
sion based on their statistical study. Ieda et al. (2008) sug-
gested that the Y-center (YAGSM = 3 ­RE) of tailward flows 
at XAGSM = −28 ­RE was displaced toward dawn by 0.3 h in 
MLT from auroral breakups. This displacement was not 
statistically significant because it was much shorter than 
the typical width of tailward flows (2 h in MLT). Nagai 
and Shinohara (2021) found that the NENL is located 
duskward of the center of the substorm current wedge; 
however, we recognize that the NENL can still be dawn-
ward of the duskside edge of the current wedge.
One interpretation of the displacement between the 
NENL and breakup is that auroral brightenings occur on 
the duskside edge of the current wedge (e.g., Amm and 
Fujii 2008; Kepko et  al. 2015). We propose an  alterna-
tive interpretation wherein the MLT of earthward flows 
decreases as they progress in the dusk. This decrease 
in MLT persists even if the streamline follows the XAGSM 
direction. The MLT further decreases if earthward flows 
curve duskward near XAGSM = −10 ­RE due to the inter-
action with the inner magnetosphere. If the NENL is 
located on the duskside, the resultant earthward flows 
on the duskside are presumably deflected further toward 
dusk near XAGSM = −10 ­RE. The MLT of earthward flows 
inside XAGSM ~ −10 ­RE is presumably close to the MLT of 
auroral breakups (McPherron et al. 2011). In summary, 
the NENL can be dawnward of the  auroral breakup, 
injection, and dipolarization.
5.6  Mercury and Earth
As explained in Sect.  1, the reconnection site is recog-
nized as being displaced toward dusk at Earth and toward 
dawn at Mercury. Here, we review two possibilities 
responsible for the dawn preference of Mercury and pro-
pose an alternative hypothesis.
One possible cause for the dawn preference is a local 
asymmetry within the thin current sheet (TCS), as pro-
posed by Liu et al. (2019). They conducted three-dimen-
sional particle-in-cell local simulations of the TCS, the 
length of which is assumed to be fixed in the dawn–dusk 
direction. The magnetic flux is transported dawnward by 
the Hall effect, as was explained in Sect. 1. They found 
that reconnection is active only in the dawnside portion 
of the TCS. In contrast, reconnection is suppressed in 
the duskside portion because an excessive removal of the 
north–south component of the magnetic field suppresses 
the generation of outward flows in the Sun–tail direction, 
ultimately preventing the further thinning of the current 
sheet to the electron scale.
This local dawn preference inside the TCS is negligi-
ble in the Earth’s magnetotail as the TCS is considerably 
shorter than the whole current sheet in the dawn–dusk 
direction. That is, both the dawnside and duskside por-
tions of the TCS tend to be located within the dusk sec-
tor. In contrast, the TCS is comparable in length to the 
whole current sheet in the Mercury’s magnetotail due 
to the short dawn–dusk length of the Mercury’s magne-
totail. That is, there is no room for the TCS to skew sig-
nificantly toward dusk; hence, the dawnside portion of 
the TCS tends to be located in the dawn and the dusk-
side portion in the dusk. For clarification, the simula-
tions explained in Sect. 1 (Lin et al. 2014; Lu et al. 2018) 
compare the thin and the thick current sheet, not the two 
portions inside the TCS.
Another possible cause is a strong southward inter-
planetary magnetic field (IMF), as proposed by Chen 
et al. (2019). They combined global MHD and local par-
ticle-in-cell simulations and found the dawn preference 
to be more evident for a stronger southward IMF. The 
background current sheet is thicker in the dawn than in 
the dusk. The strong IMF compresses the thick current 
sheet in the dawn so that the background condition is 
improved to allow for reconnection. This model requires 
the local dawn preference proposed by Liu et al. (2019).
The Chen et al. (2019) model is partly consistent with 
the following  observations. Liou et  al. (2001) found a 
weak correlation between the southward IMF and the 
MLT of auroral breakups on Earth. Ieda et  al. (2008) 
found a correlation between the MLT of auroral break-
ups on Earth and that of tailward flows (i.e., NENL). 
Thus, the MLT of the NENL is expected to correlate with 
the southward IMF. This is consistent with the results of 
Nagai and Shinohara (2022), who showed that the NENL 
in the postmidnight sector occurred under strong solar 
wind energy input conditions.
We propose an alternative hypothesis that reconnec-
tion tends to occur in the dusk at Mercury as well as at 
Earth. As discussed in Sect. 5.1, the dusk preference of 
tailward flows is robustly observed in the Earth’s magne-
totail. In contrast, the dusk preference of earthward flows 
is unclear or absent in many previous statistical studies. 
Thus, the NENL is not necessarily located on the merid-
ian where earthward flows are observed. Similarly, in the 
magnetotail of other planets the reconnection site may 
not be located on the meridian where planetward flows 

Page 13 of 17
Ieda and Miyashita ﻿Earth, Planets and Space  (2024) 76:159
	
are observed. The dawn preference of the reconnection 
site in the Mercury’s magnetotail has been inferred pri-
marily using magnetic field data (Sun et al. 2016; Smith 
et  al. 2017). This observation is subject to further con-
firmation based on thermal plasma data  that will be 
acquired in the future. Tailward-moving plasmoids may 
be displaced toward dusk in the magnetotail of Mercury.
6  Conclusions
The NENL has been widely recognized as displaced 
toward dusk (e.g., Walsh et al. 2014; Sitnov et al. 2019; 
Chen et  al. 2019; Sun et  al. 2022). The present paper 
reviewed and integrated previous observations of the 
dawn–dusk location of fast plasma flows in the near-
Earth magnetotail. We found that the dusk preference 
has been robustly reported for tailward flows but has 
been often absent for earthward flows.
These discordant observations indicate that the statisti-
cal results of earthward flows are sensitive to event selec-
tion criteria, presumably due to observational biases. 
One potential bias is caused by the fact that earthward 
flows do not necessarily originate from the NENL, but 
also from the DNL. Another potential bias is caused by 
the variability in the thickness of earthward flows.
Earthward flows were rarely observed at the time of sub-
storm onset and generally observed later. No statistical 
study has demonstrated that earthward flows are displaced 
toward dusk within 10 min of the substorm onset. We con-
clude that the dawn–dusk locations of earthward flows are 
statistically unclear at the time of substorm onset.
The results of this study suggest that reconnection in 
the near-planet magnetotail does not necessarily form 
on the meridian where planetward flows are observed. 
Hence, the tailward-moving plasmoid and the reconnec-
tion site may have been predominantly displaced toward 
dusk in the magnetotail of Mercury. This hypothesis will

Appendix A: AGSM coordinate system 
for the magnetotail
The geocentric solar magnetospheric (GSM) coordi-
nate system is often used to study the magnetosphere. 
However, the GSM system does not consider the orbital 
motion of the Earth about the Sun. The actual tail axis 
is typically 4° aberrated from the Sun–Earth axis by the 
windsock effect. Such a typical tail axis is adopted as the 
X axis in the aberrated GSM (AGSM) coordinate system 
(Fairfield 1979; Yamamoto et al. 1994; Angelopoulos et al. 
1994; Ieda et al. 1998). The AGSM system is more appro-
priate for studying the magnetotail than the GSM system 
and should be adopted for studying asymmetries relative 
to the tail axis.
Figure 8 shows the GSM coordinate system. This illus-
tration also applies to the geocentric solar ecliptic (GSE) 
coordinate system. The magnetosphere is bordered by a 
line labeled “Magnetopause”. Y = 0 corresponds to mid-
night in the tail. The positive Y region corresponds to 
dusk, which is also referred to as premidnight.
Figure 9 illustrates the aberration of the actual tail axis 
in the GSE system. On average, the solar wind flows in 
the negative XGSE direction. The Earth moves in the nega-
tive YGSE direction in an orbital motion around the Sun. 
Due to this orbital motion, the solar wind does not flow 
exactly in the negative XGSE direction but in an oblique 
direction in frames moving with the Earth. The windsock 
effect aligns the tail axis direction to the oblique solar 
wind direction. That is, the actual tail axis is not exactly 
aligned with the XGSE direction, but is aberrated.

The aberration depends on the solar wind velocity, 
as summarized in Table 2. The average orbital speed is 
generally 29.78 km/s. An aberration of 4° is often used 
(e.g., Yamamoto et  al. 1994; Ieda et  al. 1998) and cor-
responds to a solar wind speed of 426 km/s and a 2.1 ­RE 
displacement of the tail axis toward dusk at X = −30 ­RE 
at midnight.
The aberration should be considered in the GSE, not 
GSM, by definition, although we are not aware of explicit 
explanations in literature. Otherwise, the resultant ZAGSM 
location may have an artificial dawn–dusk asymmetry, 
which may have significant impacts because the plasma 
sheet is thin in the Z direction. Satellite locations and 
measured vector data should be prepared in GSE, then 
converted into aberrated GSE (AGSE), and finally into 
AGSM.
The ZAGSM location is often further corrected to repre-
sent the relative distance from the actual equatorial plane 
(i.e., neutral sheet) of the magnetotail. To emphasize this 
correction, the coordinate system was sometimes labeled 
GSM′ in the past. The simplest neutral sheet model 
assumes a hinging typically at 10 ­RE geocentric distance, 
as adopted by Yamamoto et  al. (1994) and Ieda et  al. 
(1998): The tail axis is assumed to be perpendicular to 
the geomagnetic dipole within a geocentric distance of 10 
­RE and parallel to the XAGSM axis at greater distances. A 
more refined neutral sheet model is the Tsyganenko and 
Fairfield (2004) model, as adopted by Ieda et al. (2016).
