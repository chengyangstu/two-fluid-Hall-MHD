Study of magnetic reconnection in large-scale magnetic island coalescence
via spatially coupled MHD and PIC simulations
Kirit D. Makwana,a) Rony Keppens, and Giovanni Lapenta
Centre for Mathematical Plasma Astrophysics, Department of Mathematics, KU Leuven, Celestijnenlaan
200B, 3001 Heverlee, Belgium
(Received 27 April 2018; accepted 1 August 2018; published online 23 August 2018)
We study the process of magnetic reconnection in a coalescing magnetic island setup by means of
numerical simulation. This process mimics ﬂux tube merging which can take place in the solar corona,
laboratory, and astrophysical objects. Simulations are performed with magnetohydrodynamics
(MHD), Hall-MHD, and a newly developed Coupled MHD and Particle-In-Cell (PIC) model
(CMAP). This model treats the global simulation domain with MHD, while the region around the
reconnection zone is treated with PIC. This CMAP code allows us to simulate larger-scale domains
with lesser computing power compared to fully PIC simulations. CMAP reproduces the dynamics of
fully kinetic simulations which Hall-MHD does not capture, as seen in the Hall magnetic ﬁeld and the
reconnecting current sheet structure. For large islands in kinetic simulations, the current sheet does not
form smoothly and shows chaotic behavior, and the magnetic islands also bounce and slosh. The cur-
rent sheet thickness, length, and aspect ratios are calculated. They show that in the CMAP model, the
thickness remains close to the ion skin depth, while the length changes weakly with the system size,
giving a steady aspect ratio for the two largest system size simulations. The pressure tensor also shows
large deviations from isotropy and gyrotropy near the current sheet. The CMAP simulations for
smaller system sizes are compared to fully kinetic simulations, and we ﬁnd that a minimum fraction of
area has to be provided PIC feedback in the CMAP simulations in order to produce reconnection rates
and dynamics similar to fully kinetic simulations. The reconnection rate reduces with the increasing
island size. For the CMAP model, this reduction is steeper compared to MHD and Hall-MHD initially,
but for larger system sizes, the reconnection rates in CMAP simulations show a steady behavior.

I. INTRODUCTION
Magnetic reconnection is observed or believed to occur
in a wide range of systems from Earth-based laboratories to
the heliosphere to even interstellar environments.1,2 Magnetic
reconnection involves rearrangement or change in topology
of magnetic ﬁeld lines along with a conversion of magnetic
energy into plasma kinetic or thermal energy. There are many
aspects of this process which are mysterious and subject of
active research. Some of the interesting questions are about
the rate of reconnection, the geometry of reconnection in 2D
and 3D, and the coupling of large and small scales.
The rate of reconnection is observed to depend critically
on a variety of aspects. Numerical simulations show that the
physics model used for simulations affects the rate of recon-
nection, with Hall physics playing an important role3 in
speeding up reconnection compared to purely magnetohy-
drodynamic (MHD) physics given by the Sweet-Parker
rate4,5 which itself is signiﬁcantly faster than the diffusion
rate. On the other hand, even in MHD physics, the Lundquist
number can affect the reconnection rate and dynamics. At
large Lundquist numbers in large size systems, the reconnec-
tion rate can become weakly dependent on resistivity6 and
chaotic and bursty7,8 due to secondary island formation.9,10
Also, turbulence can affect the reconnection where it can
speed up the rate of reconnection and make it independent of
the resistivity.11,12 Moreover, the rate of reconnection is not
constant but varies. In solar ﬂares, the magnetic ﬂux has to
ﬁrst build up, implying slow reconnection, before it is
released explosively in a ﬂare, implying a fast reconnection.
Reconnection can also be spontaneous like in the tearing
mode instability.13 Therefore, another aspect of reconnection

The other confounding aspect of reconnection is that it is
multi-scale and multi-physics phenomena. It can be driven on
large scales by global physics, whereas the reconnection zone
is intimately affected by small-scale physics.15 For example, in
the Earth’s magnetosphere, the solar wind driver operates at
the scales of the magnetosphere which is of 109 m, while the
plasma sheet which forms and reconnects is of the scale 107
m. Furthermore, kinetic physics plays an important role in this
reconnection where the ion skin depth scale is at 105 m and
the electron skin depth is at 103 m. Kinetic effects such as
anisotropic and agyrotropic pressures play an important role in
governing this reconnection dynamics.
The formation of current sheets is also an important pro-
cess which affects the reconnection. Here, the question is
how long current sheets can form in realistic plasmas? The
aspect ratio of a current sheet controls the rate of reconnec-
tion. Kinetic numerical studies have indicated that the length

shown that the reconnection rate is affected by the system

These studies have investigated the effect of the physics
used in simulations, with differences between MHD, Hall-
MHD, hybrid, and fully kinetic models. Previous studies
have investigated the dependence of reconnection rates on
the Lundquist number (S) in coalescence of magnetic islands
and found that they become independent of S at low S.25,26
They have also shown the dependence of the reconnection
rate on the system size. However, simulations of these prob-
lems are not easy because of the vastly different scales
involved and also the different physics. While the system
size is of the order of 100’s of di, the current sheet that forms
is barely one di and needs to be properly resolved with
kinetic physics. Also, these simulations proceed at Alfven
time scales, and so, the simulation time increases with the
system size.
To overcome multi-scale and multi-physics difﬁculties,
we present simulations with a new model of coupled MHD
and PIC domains (denoted as the CMAP model hence-
forth).28 In this model, the global simulation domain is han-
dled efﬁciently with MHD, while the region around the
reconnection zone is handled by the fully kinetic PIC
method. These two domains talk to each other both ways,
with the MHD code giving initial and boundary conditions
to PIC, whereas the PIC code constantly updates the MHD
solution. This saves computing power compared to fully
kinetic simulations as only a fraction of the domain is simu-
lated with the computationally expensive PIC model. This
allows modeling larger system sizes with limited computing
power. We brieﬂy describe some details of this two-way cou-
pling in Sec. II. It describes the problem setup of magnetic
island coalescence along with the numerical details of the
problem setup. We are able to reproduce several results that
have been found in kinetic simulations before with this
CMAP model. In Sec. III, we show a comparison of the
results produced by MHD, Hall-MHD, CMAP, and fully PIC
simulations (for smaller system sizes). We ﬁnd that the cou-
pled simulation saves computing time compared to a full
PIC simulation while capturing the relevant kinetic physics
effects which are not reproduced in MHD and Hall-MHD.
We also show the properties of the current sheet formation
and kinetic signatures near the current sheet. In Sec. IV, the
reconnection rates obtained in these simulations and how we
can get the correct reconnection rates from the coupled
MHD-PIC approach are shown. It is shown that in the
CMAP model, the reconnection rate drops as the system size
increases but reaches a steady value for island sizes larger
than 20di. Section V gives the conclusions and implications
of this study.

The island coalescence setup for smaller system sizes
(Lx  120di) is also implemented in iPIC3D. iPIC3D is a
semi-implicit moment method for PIC simulations of
plasmas.35,36 It solves a linearized version of the implicit
Ampe`re law to advance the electric ﬁeld. Then, the magnetic
ﬁeld is obtained from the induction equation, and the particles
are also moved implicitly. As a result of this semi-implicit
scheme, iPIC3D can take larger grid spacing (10–50) and
larger time steps (5–10) compared to explicit PIC codes.
The magnetic ﬁeld is initialized as given by Eq. (1) plus the
perturbation, while the electric ﬁeld is zero. The particles are
initialized with a Maxwellian distribution in each cell, having
a density given by Eq. (2), and the temperature ratio between
the two species is Ti0/Te0 ¼ 5. The mass ratio is taken to be
reduced to mi/me ¼ 25.
This setup is also simulated with a coupled MHD and
PIC model (CMAP). This model spatially couples MPI-
AMRVAC with iPIC3D, with an iPIC3D region embedded
within a global MPI-AMRVAC domain. This follows similar
coupling between MHD and PIC codes.37,38 The details of
this CMAP model can be found in Refs. 28 and 40. Brieﬂy,
in this coupling, the global domain is simulated with MPI-
AMRVAC. At some time step, a region within the global
domain is selected by the user to be simulated with iPIC3D.
In MHD to PIC coupling, information from MPI-AMRVAC
is used to provide initial conditions to iPIC3D to start the
simulation. The primitive variables of MHD, namely, density
(q), pressure (p), velocity (v), and magnetic ﬁeld (B), are sup-
plied to iPIC3D. Using this, the electromagnetic ﬁelds are
initialized. The particles are initialized with a Maxwellian dis-
tribution using the thermal velocity derived from MHD pres-
sure and the drift velocity derived from MHD velocity and
current density. After this, MPI-AMRVAC is advanced by one
time step, and the time averaged state at the interface between
MHD and PIC is supplied to iPIC3D as the boundary condi-
tions for its time step. After iPIC3D advances its state to the
next time step, moments of the distribution function are gath-
ered to obtain the MHD primitive variables. In PIC to MHD
coupling, this information from iPIC3D is used to update the
MPI-AMRVAC solution. The MHD solution is overwritten by
a weighted average of the PIC solution and the MHD solution
in the PIC domain. The weight function varies smoothly across
the interface region such that the updated MHD solution
smoothly changes from purely MHD at the edge to purely PIC
in the interior. This is the two-way coupling method.

Starting from this point of maximum current density, we

However, we do not ﬁnd similarly long current sheets in our

by Scudder,43 Aunai,44 and Swisdak.45 For the Scudder
prescription, we follow the simpliﬁed eigenvalue analysis
presented in Ref. 43 where the characteristic polynomial is
simpliﬁed by removing the eigenvalue of the pressure tensor
corresponding to the parallel direction. Qualitatively, the
three measures look very similar. They peak near the current
sheet which is at the center of the simulation domain. The
anisotropy in the pressure tensor pk=p? is also shown in Fig.

R3 and R4 CMAP simulations, but there these kinetic effects
are not located close to the center and are not smooth fea-
tures. Instead, they move around as the current sheet moves
and they are spread out unevenly around the reconnection
zone. This implies that measurements and observations of
these kinetic features of the pressure tensor need to be spread
out around the reconnection region.

These simulations show that that the reconnection rate
in large collisionless systems might become independent of
the system size. This has been seen in other simulations
also.46 It has also been observed that the reconnection rate in
a variety of kinetic simulations shows a value of 0.1 in units
of the inﬂowing plasma Alfven speed, while the outﬂow is at
the Alfven speed. This has been linked with a similar aspect
ratio of the reconnecting current sheet across these different
simulations.47,48 Although the outﬂowing plasma velocity is
not close to Alfvenic in our coalescence simulations with no
guide ﬁeld,39 we see a hint of something similar in CMAP
simulations where the current sheet aspect ratio [measured at
the time of peak reconnection, Fig. 7(c)] tapers off at larger
island sizes and so does the peak reconnection rate. This
needs to be explored more in future work going towards
larger system sizes.
V. CONCLUSIONS
We have presented simulations of the magnetic island
coalescence problem with a novel CMAP model which spa-
tially couples global MHD simulations with the embedded
PIC region. This coupling is shown to work well for this
problem setup, with its main advantage being that it saves
computing time signiﬁcantly compared to simulating the
whole domain with a kinetic model. For the cases shown
here, the CMAP simulation was typically 5 times cheaper
than the kinetic model, with the speedup being faster for
smaller PIC domains. However, we found that the PIC feed-
back has to cover at least 16% of the total simulation domain
in order to produce reconnection rates close to the fully
kinetic results. This seems to suggest that kinetic physics is
important in a much larger range beyond the immediate dif-
fusion zone. Simulations with MHD and Hall-MHD modules
of MPI-AMRVAC are also presented along with a couple of
full PIC simulations.
We ﬁnd similarities with previous work: the kinetic sim-
ulation is quite different from the Hall-MHD results. The
out-of-plane Hall magnetic ﬁeld is stronger in the CMAP
model compared to Hall-MHD simulations. The current
sheet structure is much larger in the MHD model, while it
becomes smaller in Hall-MHD. On the other hand, the
CMAP model shows a different X-point like structure of the
current sheet which matches very well with PIC results.
Magnetic island sloshing is observed in the CMAP model for
large system sizes, which is not seen in the ﬂuid simulations.
This also leads to a chaotic behavior of the reconnection
zone, with the current sheet strengthening, then weakening,

and in general moving around. The current sheet geometrical
characteristics show that the current sheet thickness in MHD
and Hall-MHD increases with the system size. On the other
hand, kinetic current sheets remain at the ion skin depth scale
independent of the system size. This corroborates with other
results of kinetic scale current sheets49,50 which have their
thickness close to the skin depth scale. However, this does
not necessarily imply that the ion physics sets the current
sheet thickness. Other studies have identiﬁed the electron
meandering length scale51 as a possible physics setting cur-
rent sheet thickness. We calculate the electron meandering
length scale by calculating the distance from the X-point
along the x axis where the electron thermal gyroradius
becomes comparable to this distance. This meandering
length scale is around 1de, giving a current sheet thickness of
2de. However, due to the limited mass ratio and resolution, it
is difﬁcult to separate clearly this length scale from the ion-
skin depth. Further scaling studies with higher mass ratios
can clarify this issue. The MHD and Hall-MHD also show
that the lengths of the current sheets grow almost linearly
with the system size, whereas the lengths in the CMAP simu-
lations grow weakly. As a result of this, the aspect ratio of
the current sheet very closely matches between the three dif-
ferent simulation models. This aspect ratio increases as the
system size gets larger. However, for the largest system size,
the CMAP model shows that the aspect ratio steadies to
between 10 and 20.
The CMAP model allows us to calculate the reconnec-
tion rates for large system sizes. The reconnection rate is
also calculated for the different simulations, and their scal-
ings are shown. Initially, the CMAP reconnection rate drops
rapidly as the system size gets larger, compared to the drop
in the reconnection rate shown by the ﬂuid models.
However, for islands larger than k > 20di, the peak recon-
nection rate steadies at a value close to 0.2. The average
reconnection rate also shows a steady value, although this
may depend on the length of time averaging. This work indi-
cates that the aspect ratio of the reconnection zone in kinetic
simulations becomes constant at large enough system sizes,
giving a similar maximum reconnection rate for different
system sizes. Future work with even larger simulations will
be needed to clarify this.
