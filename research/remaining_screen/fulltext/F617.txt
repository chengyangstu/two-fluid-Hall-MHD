                           tosphere is highly variable [4] and strongly dependent on upstream solar wind [5] and

          IMF [6, 7]. The mini-magnetosphere is shaped by both these external conditions [8] and
              contributions from crustal magnetic fields [9].

             The induced magnetosphere forms because the solar wind interacts directly with Mars’
              ionosphere. The supersonic solar wind from upstream of Mars first encounters the plan­
               etary conducting layer and the planetary ions from the ionosphere mass-load the solar
            wind and slow the flow. At the same time, the IMF is compressed and draped around the
              ionospheric obstacle [11]. As the draped magnetic field is carried closer to Mars, it piles
            up above the ionosphere and forms the magnetic pileup region [1, 12]. The outer bound­
              ary of the induced magnetosphere is often associated with the magnetic pileup boundary
                 [12], where the plasma composition and magnetic field properties change from solar-
             wind-dominated magnetosheath plasma to planetary-ion-rich induced magnetospheric
             plasma [13]. Boundaries between different regions on dayside induced magnetosphere
               are shown in Fig. 2. This process also creates an extended induced magnetotail on the
               nightside. In the magnetotail, the draped magnetic field is stretched anti-sunward into
            two lobes of oppositely directed magnetic field separated by a cross-tail current sheet
                 [14]. The global position of the magnetotail current sheet also varies systematically with
              the radial IMF orientation, demonstrating an additional upstream control on the large-
               scale tail configuration [15].

             The Mars’ ionosphere is both a boundary of the induced magnetosphere and the main
              source of planetary ions. On the dayside, it is produced primarily by photoionization
               of the neutral upper atmosphere by solar EUV radiation [17]. On the nightside, where
               direct solar illumination is absent, ionization is maintained largely by plasma transport
               [18] and energetic electron precipitation [19, 20]. The ionosphere is dominated by heavy
             molecular and atomic ions, especially CO2+, O2+, and O+ ions. And these ions can be
              supplied to the induced magnetosphere and magnetotail.

             The presence of strong remanent crustal magnetic fields, mainly concentrated in the
             southern highlands (Fig. 3), is one of the distinctive features at Mars. These crustal mag­
               netic anomalies form localized closed-loop systems or open magnetic field lines that
             can extend into the surrounding plasma environment (Fig. 4) [23]. They can modify the
             draped IMF [24–26], shield or trap ionospheric plasmas [27, 28], and guide electron
               precipitation [29]. As a result, the crustal magnetic fields provide additional magnetic
             boundaries and complex magnetic topologies around Mars. Recent statistical mapping
               further shows that the global distribution of magnetic field topologies in the Martian
             induced magnetosphere is systematically organized by the solar-wind convective electric
                 field [30].



                 1.2  Magnetic reconnection physics and ion dynamics
             Magnetic reconnection is a process in which magnetic field lines change their connectiv­
                  ity in a localized non-ideal region, enabling magnetic energy to be converted into plasma
              bulk motion, heating, and particle acceleration [32–35] (Fig. 5). In ideal magnetohydro­
             dynamics (MHD), magnetic field lines are frozen into the plasma and magnetic topology
                   is conserved. Reconnection occurs when this frozen-in condition breaks down, typically
              within thin current sheets where kinetic, resistive, or collisional effects become impor­
               tant [36]. The newly reconnected magnetic field lines are then accelerated away from
              the reconnection site by magnetic tension, producing bidirectional plasma outflows with
             speeds of the order of the local Alfvén speed.

                In  collisionless reconnection, the breakdown of the frozen-in condition occurs
             through the decoupling of ions and electrons. Because ions have larger inertial lengths

            and gyroradii than electrons, especially CO2 +, O2+, and O+ ions, and these ions decouple
            from the magnetic field on ion scales, forming an ion diffusion region [38], while elec­
              trons remain magnetized until smaller electron scales are reached. This two-scale struc­
                ture, consisting of an ion diffusion region and an embedded electron diffusion region
             [39–42] shown in Fig. 6, is a central feature of collisionless reconnection. Ion–electron
             decoupling also produces Hall currents and Hall magnetic perturbations, which are
           among the most widely used in-situ diagnostics of reconnection in planetary plasma
             environments [43–48].

               Ion dynamics are central to the interpretation of reconnection observations. In the
              ion diffusion region and reconnection exhaust, ions can deviate from ideal E×B drift,
           become demagnetized, and gain energy from reconnection electric fields, Hall electric
                  fields, and motional electric fields associated with reconnected magnetic flux. Reconnec­
               tion outflows, ion beams, non-Maxwellian velocity distributions, and ion temperature
            enhancements are therefore important signatures of magnetic-to-particle energy con­
               version. In addition, secondary structures such as magnetic islands or plasmoids [50],
                flux ropes [51, 52], and reconnection fronts [53–56] can further energize ions through
               trapping, contraction, merging, and betatron- or Fermi-type acceleration [57, 58].

               For Mars, ion dynamics are expected to be important and different from Earth because
              the plasma is often composed not only of solar-wind protons but also of planetary heavy
                ions. Protons and heavy ions may not demagnetize or respond to reconnection electric
                 fields in the same way, so reconnection at Mars often needs to be interpreted as a multi-
              ion process rather than as a simple electron–proton system. This makes ion composition
              a key parameter for understanding reconnection outflows, energy partition, and atmo­
              spheric ion escape at Mars. Besides, collisionality is another important factor. Reconnec­
               tion in Earth’s magnetosphere is generally expected to be collisionless. In Mars’ lower
              ionosphere, however, ion-neutral and electron-neutral collisions become increasingly
             important and these collisions can modify the reconnection process. Therefore, Mars
              provides a natural environment in which reconnection may transition from collision­
                 less behavior at higher altitudes to partially collisional behavior at lower ionospheric
                altitudes.
                This review summarizes recent advances of magnetic reconnection in the Mars’ space
             environment. We first outline the reconnection physics and Mars’ plasma conditions
            most relevant to the observations. In the following sections, we review recent studies
               of reconnection in different regions of Mars, followed by a discussion of its effects on
             magnetospheric structure, ion escape, and heavy-ion reconnection physics. Finally, we
            summarize the current understanding of reconnection in Mars’ space environment and
             propose several open questions for future studies.

                2  Magnetic reconnection in Mars’ space environment
                 2.1  Reconnection in the induced magnetosphere
            The induced magnetopause (IMP), the outer boundary of the Mars’ induced magne­
               tosphere, separates shocked solar-wind plasma in the magnetosheath from the heavy-
               ion-rich plasma in the induced magnetosphere ([13, 59, 60]. Wang, Yu [61] presented
                in-situ evidence from MAVEN observations for magnetic reconnection at the dayside
          IMP as shown in Fig. 7. The event showed a clear transition from magnetosheath plasma
               to planetary-ion-dominated plasma across the boundary, together with magnetic signa­
               tures more consistent with a rotational discontinuity than with a purely tangential one.

                Hall-like magnetic perturbations were also detected within the current sheet, and a sun­
            ward outflow jet was observed that contained both solar-wind protons and planetary
             heavy ions. These features suggest that the dayside induced magnetosphere can become
              magnetically open to the solar wind through reconnection.

                This picture also implies that reconnection at the IMP is asymmetric, owing to the
               large contrast in plasma density and ion composition across the boundary (Fig. 7). On
            one side lies shocked proton-dominated solar-wind plasma, whereas on the other lies a
             denser planetary plasma dominated by heavy ions. Such asymmetry is expected to affect
              current-sheet structure, Hall-field geometry, and the relative acceleration of light and
             heavy ion species during reconnection [61, 62]. More broadly, IMP reconnection sug­
               gests that the dayside boundary cannot always be treated as an impermeable interface,

             but may instead provide a direct pathway for plasma exchange and, potentially, for plan­
               etary ion loss through direct acceleration or subsequent solar-wind pickup.
              Magnetic reconnection relevant to the near-Mars environment is not restricted to
             Martian plasma boundaries. MAVEN has recently provided direct evidence of Petschek-
              type reconnection exhausts within solar-wind current sheets near Mars, characterized
             by bifurcated magnetic-field profiles and Alfvenic ion [63]. The unusually broad exhaust
              suggests that reconnection can restructure and broaden upstream current sheets before
              they encounter the Martian bow shock. Such upstream preconditioning may modify the
             magnetic shear and plasma conditions incident on the induced magnetosphere, although
                   its quantitative influence on IMP reconnection remains to be established.

                 2.2  Reconnection in the induced magnetotail
               Similar to the magnetotail of Earth, Mars’ induced magnetotail geometry provides favor­
               able conditions for magnetic reconnection. Early evidence for magnetotail reconnection
           came from Mars Global Surveyor measurements, which revealed Hall-like magnetic sig­
              natures and electron pitch-angle features suggestive of reconnection diffusion regions
                [64, 65]. With the measurements from MAVEN, the observational picture became
          much clearer. Harada, Halekas [66] reported one of the first comprehensive event stud­
                 ies showing signatures consistent with collisionless reconnection in the near-Mars tail,
              including Hall magnetic perturbations, ion outflows involving both solar-wind pro­
              tons and planetary heavy ions, and electron distributions indicative of altered magnetic
              topology (Fig. 8). Later work refined the observational criteria and showed that recon­
              nection can be identified through a combination of current-sheet structure, Hall fields,
              multi-species ion jets, electron pitch-angle topology, and, where possible, Walén con­
               sistency of the outflow [67, 68]. These observations indicate that fully developed colli­
               sionless reconnection can occur in the Mars’ magnetotail despite the absence of a global
                intrinsic dipole.

                   Statistical studies further suggest that magnetotail reconnection at Mars is not rare.
             Harada, Halekas [67] found that Hall-field signatures of reconnection occur prefer­
                entially in the near-Mars tail and show a pronounced hemispheric asymmetry, with
           more events detected in the − E hemisphere, in which the solar wind motional electric
                 field points toward the planet (Fig. 9). A larger survey by [69], using both Hall signa­
               tures and fast proton flows, concluded that reconnecting current sheets make up a sub­
                stantial fraction of Mars’ magnetotail current-sheet crossings (Fig. 10). It showed that
              reconnecting sheets tend to be thinner than non-reconnecting ones, with characteristic
              thicknesses comparable to the local proton inertial scale, a condition favorable for fast
                collisionless reconnection [70, 71].

               Coordinated MAVEN and Tianwen-1 observations further connect near-tail recon­
              nection with downstream current-sheet dynamics. Wen, Halekas [72] found that
              approximately two-thirds of the magnetotail-flapping intervals observed by Tianwen-1
              coincided with reconnection signatures detected by MAVEN, and several intervals con­
              tained multiple reconnection-generated flux ropes. They proposed that tailward-prop­
              agating flux ropes perturb the downstream plasma and drive instabilities that initiate
              current-sheet flapping. The association is statistically strong but should be described as

          A distinctive feature of Martian magnetotail reconnection is the strong presence of
              planetary heavy ions, especially O+ and O2 +, in addition to solar-wind protons. This
              multi-ion composition modifies the kinetic physics of reconnection relative to Earth’s
           more proton-dominated plasma sheet. Observations consistently show that proton out­
              flows are more strongly accelerated than heavy-ion outflows, reflecting the fact that
                lighter ions remain more effectively coupled to proton-scale reconnection structures,
             whereas heavier ions are weakly magnetized and respond differently to the reconnec­
               tion electric field [49, 66, 67]. Statistical results likewise suggest that reconnecting cur­
               rent sheets are associated with enhanced tailward proton fluxes, whereas the response
               of heavy ions is less systematic [69]. This suggests that magnetotail reconnection likely
              contributes directly to proton loss and may also affect heavy-ion escape under specific
               conditions, for example through flux-rope formation, topology change, or indirect ener­
               gization pathways [68, 73].
               Planetward ion populations provide an additional, although non-unique, diagnostic of
             plasma transport in the Martian magnetotail. Global MAVEN flux maps reveal substan­
                   tial inward as well as outward planetary-ion fluxes, while surveys of current-sheet cross­
               ings show that sunward or planetward ion jets are widespread and generally embedded
               in magnetic configurations consistent with reconnection [74, 75]. During the Decem­
              ber 2022 disappearing-solar-wind event, high-energy heavy-ion fluxes decreased while
             low-energy fluxes increased; plume escape weakened but the net tailward escape flux
              increased [76], demonstrating that upstream dynamic pressure can strongly reorganize
              ion transport without establishing a one-to-one correspondence between planetward
              flow and reconnection.

                 2.3  Reconnection in crustal field regions
             Mars’ crustal anomalies interact directly with the draped IMF and the shocked solar
            wind [3, 77], which provides favorable conditions for magnetic reconnection.

               Current observations indicated that reconnection in crustal-field regions occurs in
               several geometries. One is reconnection at the mini-magnetopause, where closed crustal
             magnetic fields interact with the antiparallel draped IMF [78, 79]. These events occur at
              the boundary between the shocked solar wind and crustal-field-supported mini-magne­
               tospheres, and they share some features with boundary reconnection elsewhere in plan­
               etary plasma environments while remaining strongly influenced by local crustal topology
            and ionospheric plasma conditions (Fig. 11). A second geometry involves reconnection
            between neighboring crustal loops. Based on MAVEN observations, Chen, Huang [80]
             proposed that adjacent crustal loops can be sheared by solar-wind-driven flow, particu­
                 larly near the terminator, where thin current layers may form and reconnect (Fig. 12).
               Lin, Huang [81] reported observations of interchange reconnection between open and

               Reconnection in crustal-field regions has several important consequences for the
             Mars’ plasma environment. First, it can open previously closed magnetic loops and
              thereby provide pathways for plasma exchange between the ionosphere and the sur­
             rounding solar-wind-driven environment [78–80]. Second, crustal-field reconnection is
                closely linked to discrete aurora. Statistical analysis of MAVEN observations shows that
            most discrete auroral events above the strongest southern crustal anomalies occur under
                 field configurations favorable for antiparallel reconnection between crustal fields and
              the draped IMF (Fig. 14) [84], supporting the view that reconnection produces the open
             magnetic geometry and field-aligned electron acceleration needed for auroral precipita­
               tion [85–87]. Third, crustal-field reconnection may influence larger-scale magnetic con­
               nectivity by contributing to plasma transport into mini-magnetospheres and possibly to
              the broader magnetic configuration of the Martian tail and wake [24, 27, 88]. Although
              the quantitative importance of these effects remains uncertain, recent observations
               indicate that crustal-field reconnection is a significant component of Mars–solar-wind
              coupling.



                 2.4  Reconnection in the ionosphere
             Mars’ ionosphere serves both as the main source of planetary heavy ions and as a plasma
             regime that spans collisionless, weakly collisional, and strongly collisional conditions.
              Observational evidence indicates that magnetic reconnection can occur in the upper
             Mars’ ionosphere in both strong and weak crustal-field settings. Above crustal-field
               regions, MAVEN observations reported by Ye, Xu [82] and Man, Xu [83] showed signa­
               tures consistent with collisionless reconnection, including thin current sheets, topology
               transitions inferred from suprathermal electrons, Hall-like magnetic perturbations, ion
              outflow jets, and localized ion energization. An important more recent development is
              the identification of reconnection in the dayside upper ionosphere above weak crustal-
                 field regions. Using coordinated Tianwen-1 and MAVEN observations, Lin, Huang [89]
              reported a current-sheet crossing below the ion composition boundary that exhib­
                ited magnetic reversal, Hall-like magnetic structure, ion outflow approaching the local
             Alfvén speed, and a clear transition from closed field lines below the sheet to draped
            IMF-connected topology above it (Fig. 15). The event was linked to an upstream IMF
               rotation observed by Tianwen-1, suggesting that ionospheric reconnection is not only

              confined to strongly magnetized crustal regions and may also be triggered more broadly
             by external magnetic forcing.

          A distinctive aspect of reconnection in the Martian ionosphere is the role of collisions
            between charged particles and neutrals. As altitude decreases, increasing neutral density
             enhances ion-neutral and electron-neutral collisional coupling, which can suppress Hall
               physics, reduce outflow speed, and slow reconnection relative to the collisionless upper
             ionosphere [90]. This distinguishes the ionosphere from the weakly collisional plasma
             environments of the Martian magnetotail and outer induced magnetosphere. Cravens,
             Fowler [90] argued that the transition between collisionless and collisionally damped
             reconnection occurs near an altitude of about 300 km on the dayside, with efficient
               X-line reconnection favored above this level and increasingly suppressed reconnection
             below it. More recent statistical work by Harada, Cravens [91] supports this picture,
             showing that reliable ion-jet signatures are much more commonly identified in the col­
                lisionless regime than in the collisional one (Fig. 16). Even near the transition region,
             observed outflows tend to be sub-Alfvénic, consistent with partial damping by ion-neu­
                 tral friction.

              These ionospheric processes have important implications for planetary ion escape
             because they can convert cold, thermal ionospheric ions into energized, field-aligned
               outflows. This is particularly significant for heavy ions such as O+ and O2+, which dom­
               inate much of the Martian ionosphere but require substantial energization to escape.

             Recent event studies suggest that reconnection in the upper ionosphere can generate
               transient ion escape fluxes that are locally large compared with background outflow
              channels [89]. More broadly, ionospheric reconnection may act as a pre-acceleration
              process that feeds ions into larger-scale escape pathways, including transport into the
              magnetotail and pickup by the solar wind. Because this process can be externally driven
             by IMF changes, its long-term significance likely depends on solar-wind variability and
           may have been greater in the early Solar System, when both solar activity and atmo­
              spheric forcing were stronger.

                 2.5  IMF control of reconnection at Mars
             Because Mars lacks a global intrinsic dipole field, the IMF provides the dominant exter­
               nal control on magnetic reconnection throughout the Martian plasma environment. As
              the IMF is compressed and draped around the planet, its orientation and temporal vari­
                 ability strongly influence where antiparallel field configurations develop, how current
              sheets are formed, and when reconnection is triggered. In this sense, reconnection at
            Mars is not controlled primarily by an internal global magnetic geometry, as at Earth,
             but by the coupling between upstream IMF conditions, induced magnetic topology, and
               localized crustal fields.

               Evidence for this control has strengthened considerably in recent years. Early obser­
               vations established that reconnection occurs in the Martian plasma environment,
               especially in the magnetotail [64], but more recent multipoint measurements have
            gone further by linking upstream IMF variability directly to reconnection onset. Using
             upstream observations from Tianwen-1 together with  in-situ measurements from
          MAVEN, Wen, Halekas [68] reported a magnetotail reconnection event that followed
              a rapid IMF rotation by about two minutes, suggesting that upstream field rotations can
              reorganize the induced magnetotail and create favorable conditions for reconnection
                 (Fig. 17). A similar coupling was reported in the dayside upper ionosphere, where Lin,
           Huang [89] observed reconnection signatures shortly after an IMF rotation measured
             upstream (Figs. 15 and 18). These studies both indicate that transient IMF variations can
                trigger reconnection in both the magnetotail and the dayside ionosphere.

             The physical picture that emerges is that IMF rotations modify the draping pattern
            around Mars and can rapidly change the local magnetic shear at boundaries and within
              the induced magnetosphere (Fig. 18). This process can produce favorable antiparallel
               configurations, open magnetic field lines, and accelerate both solar-wind and planetary
                ions. In this way, IMF variability appears to link upstream solar-wind dynamics directly
               to ionospheric restructuring, magnetotail activity, and transient plasma escape from
             Mars. At the same time, the global significance of IMF-controlled reconnection remains
               uncertain. Most existing evidence still comes from a limited number of well-character­
              ized events, and the relative importance of IMF forcing compared with local magnetic
               topology, crustal fields, and internal current-sheet evolution is not yet fully resolved.
              Nevertheless, current observations suggest that IMF variability is a major control on
           when and where reconnection occurs at Mars, and that its influence may have been even
               greater under the stronger and more variable solar-wind conditions of early Mars.

                 2.6  Numerical modeling perspectives on magnetic reconnection at Mars
             Numerical studies provide an important complement to the  in-situ observations
             reviewed above because they help link local reconnection signatures to the global solar
            wind–Mars interaction. Although direct numerical studies dedicated specifically to
             magnetic reconnection at Mars are still relatively limited, recent MHD, Hall-MHD, and
              hybrid investigations have significantly improved the physical context in which Martian
             reconnection should be understood [31, 94–96]. In particular, these studies show that
             reconnection at Mars cannot be treated as an isolated local process, but instead develops
              within a highly structured induced magnetosphere controlled by IMF draping, crustal
             magnetic topology, planetary heavy-ion mass loading, and, at low altitudes, ionospheric
                 collisionality.
              At the global scale, numerical models have shown that the Martian plasma environ­
           ment is highly sensitive to the geometry of the external and internal magnetic fields.
              Simulations of the solar wind interaction with rotating crustal magnetic fields indicate
               that the large-scale magnetic topology around Mars is not static but varies with the rela­
                tive configuration between the solar wind and the crustal-field system [31]. This result is
               relevant to reconnection because such global topological changes can alter where strong
             magnetic shear and thin current layers are most likely to form [31, 97]. More broadly,
               multifluid MHD simulations also demonstrate that heavy-ion mass loading from the
             upper atmosphere and exosphere substantially modifies the large-scale interaction [95].
             For example, three-dimensional thermosphere–exosphere inputs are required to repro­
             duce key features of the Martian ionosphere, and hot exospheric oxygen can suppress
              molecular-ion escape through the mass loading produced by high-altitude O+ ions [95].
             These results imply that reconnection at Mars occurs in a strongly composition-depen­
             dent background plasma, and that the efficiency of reconnection-driven energization
            and escape should depend on both upstream forcing and planetary-ion supply.
             The most direct numerical step toward reconnection physics at Mars is provided by a
               global Hall-MHD model with an embedded PIC treatment of the near-tail current sheet
                 [94]. Such a model is especially important because it bridges the gap between the global
              induced-magnetotail structure and kinetic-scale reconnection physics. In this frame­
             work, the global draped-tail geometry supplies the reconnecting current sheet, while
              the embedded kinetic description resolves the ion- and electron-scale structure of the
             reconnection region [94]. This multiscale approach is qualitatively consistent with the
         MAVEN observations summarized above, which show Hall-like magnetic perturba­
                tions, ion outflows involving both protons and planetary heavy ions, and stronger and
           more systematically organized acceleration of protons than of heavy planetary ions in
              reconnecting Martian current sheets [63, 64, 66]. The agreement indicates that the Hall-
                 like signatures observed by MAVEN can be interpreted as local kinetic manifestations
           embedded in a globally formed induced-magnetotail current sheet, rather than as struc­
               tures generated independently of the large-scale solar wind–Mars interaction.
                In addition, global multifluid models of Martian ionospheric electrodynamics indicate
               that neutral collisions become increasingly important at lower altitudes, particularly in
              the dynamo region and near crustal-field cusps and loops [98, 99]. More generally, mul­
                 tifluid simulations of magnetic reconnection in partially ionized plasmas demonstrate
               that ion–neutral coupling, ionization, and recombination can substantially modify cur­
               rent-sheet evolution and reconnection dynamics [100]. These results provide a physical

===== PDF PAGE 21 =====
Lin et al. Discover Space      (2026) 130:17                                                                       Page 21 of 29



            framework for interpreting the observational transition from more Alfvénic, Hall-like
              upper-ionospheric events to weaker or more strongly damped signatures deeper in the
             Martian ionosphere.
              These simulations and observations support a multiscale picture in which global solar-
            wind forcing, IMF draping, and crustal topology prepare reconnection-prone current
               sheets, whereas local Hall physics, heavy-ion composition, and collisional effects gov­
             ern the detailed energy conversion and plasma response. A major challenge for future
            work is therefore to develop self-consistent models that simultaneously include realistic
               crustal fields, multi-ion chemistry, ionospheric collisions, kinetic reconnection physics,
            and time-dependent upstream driving [94, 95].

                3  Discussions
                 3.1  Reconfiguration of Mars’ magnetosphere by magnetic reconnection
              In contrast to Earth, where reconnection operates within a global dipolar system, recon­
              nection at Mars develops in an induced magnetosphere shaped by solar-wind draping,
               localized crustal magnetic fields, and a heavy-ion-rich ionosphere. Its structural effects
               are therefore expressed less through classical dipolar substorm behavior and more
             through the formation, evolution, and disruption of thin current sheets, flux ropes,
              reconnection-driven jets, and front-like structures. These recent observations show
               that Mars’ magnetosphere is not simply a steady draped-field configuration, but a highly
            dynamic system that is repeatedly reorganized by reconnection [101].
          A global consequence of reconnection is the production of magnetic flux ropes, which
              represent coherent bundles of reconnected flux and an important mode of magnetic-flux
            and plasma transport. In the Martian magnetotail, MGS observations revealed chains
               of flux ropes moving tailward within the current sheet, suggesting that a single recon­
              nection site can generate multiple detached structures through secondary instability or
             time-dependent reconnection [102]. Statistical studies further suggest that ionospheric
                flux ropes may arise through more than one pathway, including reconnection between
               crustal loops and reconnection between crustal fields and the IMF [103, 104]. Structur­
                   ally, these observations show that reconnection at Mars does not merely alter field-line
               connectivity locally, but can create detached or semi-detached magnetic entities that
               redistribute both flux and planetary plasma across the magnetosphere.
               Reconnection-driven  jets and front-like structures provide further evidence that
             reconnection actively reorganizes Mars’ induced magnetosphere. Bidirectional ion jets
             observed within current sheets on both the dayside and nightside indicate local con­
              version of magnetic energy into directed plasma flow and mark the release of mag­
               netic tension from newly reconnected field lines [74]. Their broad distribution suggests
               that reconnection-driven flow channels are a common component of Martian plasma
               transport. The detection of a tailward reconnection front in the Martian magnetotail
             extended this picture further by showing that sharp front-like magnetic structures can
            form even in an induced magnetotail without a global intrinsic dipole field [105]. Such
               fronts indicate that reconnection at Mars can generate organized propagating structures
               that redistribute flux and plasma at ion scales, much as dipolarization-like fronts do in
                intrinsic magnetospheres, but within a markedly different global magnetic geometry.
              These observations support a view of Mars’ magnetosphere as a reconnection-driven,
                structurally variable system. Reconnection thins and fragments current sheets, generates

===== PDF PAGE 22 =====
Lin et al. Discover Space      (2026) 130:17                                                                       Page 22 of 29



                flux ropes and ejected magnetic structures, and drives jets and fronts that redistribute
             magnetic flux and plasma. It links kinetic-scale processes to larger-scale reconfiguration
               of the induced magnetosphere. These structural effects also help set the conditions for
             plasma escape and atmospheric loss. Global MAVEN maps of electromagnetic energy
              transport and conversion place these local reconnection processes within the planetary-
               scale energy budget: the bow shock acts predominantly as a generator, the induced mag­
                netotail as a load, and the magnetosheath alternates between these roles depending on
              the draping geometry [106]. This system-level picture provides the macroscopic context
               in which local reconnection can contribute to plasma heating, directed acceleration,
             magnetic reconfiguration, and atmospheric escape, although the fraction of the total
             energy conversion attributable specifically to reconnection has not yet been isolated.

                 3.2  Contributions of magnetic reconnection on ion escape from Mars
             Magnetic reconnection likely affects Martian ion escape in two closely coupled ways: by
             changing magnetic topology and by energizing planetary plasma. At Mars, ion escape
                   is not only determined solely by local acceleration, but also by whether ions can access
             magnetic pathways that connect the ionosphere to the magnetosheath, the magnetotail,
              or interplanetary space. Reconnection is therefore important not only as a energy-con­
              version process, but also as a transport mechanism that opens, closes, and reorganizes
             escape channels. Therefore, reconnection-driven escape at Mars should be understood
               as the combined outcome of magnetic linking, ion heating, and subsequent large-scale
             plasma transport.
          On the dayside, reconnection at the induced magnetopause and in crustal-field regions
              appears capable of providing direct access between ionospheric plasma and the exter­
               nal solar-wind-driven environment. Dayside induced-magnetosphere reconnection can
            open the boundary between the induced magnetosphere and the magnetosheath, allow­
              ing planetary ions to cross into regions where they may be lost through direct accelera­
               tion or subsequent pickup by the solar wind [107, 108]. Similarly, in crustal-field regions,
             reconnection between crustal magnetic fields and the draped IMF can convert previ­
              ously closed loops into open field lines and thereby provide pathways for plasma release
            from near-ionospheric source regions [78]. Reconnection at mini-magnetopause bound­
                aries or in more eruptive crustal-field events may drive localized plasma release on top
               of this basic opening process. These dayside reconnection are potentially important for
              ion escape, even if their global contribution remains difficult to quantify from the cur­
               rently limited event statistics.
          On the nightside, magnetotail reconnection provides a complementary escape path­
              way. In the induced magnetotail, reconnection converts magnetic energy into tailward
             plasma flow, restructures the cross-tail current sheet, and generates ion jets, flux ropes,
            and reconnection fronts that transport plasma away from the planet. Statistical stud­
                 ies suggest that reconnecting current sheets are associated with enhanced tailward pro­
             ton fluxes, indicating that magnetotail reconnection likely contributes directly to proton
               escape. The role of tail reconnection in heavy-ion loss appears more complex. Statistical
               studies indicate that heavy ions can be heated and transported in reconnecting struc­
                tures, but their statistical response is less systematic than that of protons [67, 69]. This
                 likely reflects the multicomponent nature of the Martian plasma sheet, where heavy ions
               are only weakly magnetized relative to proton-scale current sheets and may therefore

===== PDF PAGE 23 =====
Lin et al. Discover Space      (2026) 130:17                                                                       Page 23 of 29



             couple less efficiently to reconnection electric fields. Magnetotail reconnection is thus
              best viewed as an important escape channel whose effectiveness depends on both mag­
               netic structure and plasma composition.
             The ionosphere itself may also contribute directly to escape through reconnection,
                particularly in the upper ionosphere where collisionless or weakly collisional reconnec­
               tion can accelerate cold planetary ions into field-aligned outflows [90]. In this setting,
              ionospheric reconnection may function less as an isolated escape mechanism and more
               as a pre-acceleration stage that feeds ions into larger-scale escape pathways, including
              transport into the magnetotail or pickup by the solar wind. This is especially relevant
                for heavy ions from Mars, which dominate much of Mars’ ionosphere but require sub­
                stantial energization before they can escape. By contrast, at lower altitudes increas­
              ing ion-neutral collisionality is expected to reduce outflow speeds and suppress direct
              reconnection-driven escape. The efficiency of ionospheric reconnection as an escape
              process therefore depends strongly on altitude and collisional regime.
                 Overall, recent observations suggest that reconnection contributes to Martian ion
             escape through multiple pathways rather than through a single dominant channel.
                  Its effects include opening the dayside boundary, releasing plasma from crustal-field
               regions, driving tailward outflow in the magnetotail, and pre-accelerating thermal ions
               in the upper ionosphere. Reconnection is also likely to become more effective during
              periods of strong IMF variability or disturbed solar-wind forcing, when favorable mag­
               netic shear and rapid magnetospheric reconfiguration occur more often. For early Mars,
           when the young Sun likely drove a denser and more variable solar wind, such reconnec­
              tion-driven escape may have been substantially enhanced. However, the cumulative con­
               tribution of reconnection to the long-term Martian ion loss budget remains uncertain,
                particularly in comparison with other escape channels such as pickup, sputtering, and
             ambipolar outflow. A central task for future work is therefore to determine when recon­
              nection merely redistributes plasma within the Martian system and when it produces
              net loss to interplanetary space.

                 3.3  Heavy-ion effects on magnetic reconnection
         A defining property of magnetic reconnection at Mars is that it often develops in a
             plasma environment rich in planetary heavy ions rather than in a predominantly pro­
             ton plasma. Mars’ ionosphere is dominated by heavy ions such as O+, O2+, and CO2+,
            which also populate the induced magnetosphere, crustal-field regions, and magnetotail
              current sheet [10, 109]. Reconnection at Mars differs in an important respect from the
           more proton-dominated reconnection environments typically discussed for Earth’s mag­
              netosphere. In this sense, Mars provides a natural laboratory for examining how heavy
              ions modify collisionless reconnection.
             The most basic effect of heavy ions is mass loading. By increasing the plasma mass
                density, heavy ions reduce the characteristic Alfvén speed and therefore modify recon­
              nection outflow speeds, current-sheet scales, and energy conversion rates [110]. In a
              multi-ion plasma, protons and heavy ions are not expected to demagnetize in the same
            way or on the same scales. This introduces a more complex kinetic hierarchy than in the
              standard electron–proton picture, in which the ion diffusion region is usually charac­
               terized by a single dominant ion scale. In principle, a heavy-ion-rich plasma can sup­
              port broader ion-scale structure, altered Hall fields, and species-dependent exhaust

===== PDF PAGE 24 =====
Lin et al. Discover Space      (2026) 130:17                                                                       Page 24 of 29



             dynamics. Although the full multiscale diffusion-region hierarchy predicted by general­
              ized multi-fluid or kinetic theory has not yet been resolved directly at Mars, previous
              observations are broadly consistent with the idea that heavy ions modify both the struc­
               ture and the efficiency of reconnection [67, 69].
             The clearest observational evidence for this effect comes from the induced magneto­
                    tail. MAVEN observations show that proton outflows in reconnecting current sheets
               are generally stronger and more clearly organized than those of heavy ions, whereas
              the response of O+ and O2+ is weaker and more variable [66, 67, 69]. This behavior is
               consistent with the expectation that lighter ions remain more effectively coupled to pro­
               ton-scale reconnection structures, while heavier ions, with larger inertial lengths and
                gyroradii, are less efficiently accelerated by the same electric fields. Similar asymmetry
           may also affect boundary reconnection and upper-ionospheric reconnection, where the
             plasma on one side of the current sheet is often strongly enriched in planetary heavy
                ions. In such cases, reconnection should be regarded not simply as asymmetric in den­
                   sity, but also as asymmetric in ion composition.

                4  Knowns and unknowns
             Recent studies, based on measurements from MGS, Mars Express, MAVEN, and Tian­
             wen-1, have shown that reconnection can occur in multiple regions, including the
              dayside induced magnetosphere [61, 78], the induced magnetotail [66], crustal-field
              regions [78–81], and the upper ionosphere [78, 82]. Across these regions, reconnection
             can restructure current sheets, generate flux ropes and reconnection fronts, acceler­
               ate plasma, open magnetic escape pathways, and influence auroral activity and ion loss.
             Reconnection at Mars differs from more familiar terrestrial cases because of its induced
               global topology, strong crustal-field asymmetry, partial ionospheric collisionality, and
               substantial planetary heavy-ion component.
               Despite these advances, several key questions still remain unresolved.
             The occurrence rate of reconnection in different Martian regions is still not well quan­
                  tified. Magnetotail reconnection has been studied statistically, but dayside induced-
             magnetosphere reconnection, crustal-field reconnection, and ionospheric reconnection
               are still largely constrained by limited events. Future statistical surveys should deter­
            mine how reconnection occurrence depends on solar zenith angle, altitude, crustal-field
               strength, local time, solar cycle, IMF orientation, and upstream solar-wind conditions.
             The contribution of reconnection to the global ion escape budget remains uncertain.
             Reconnection can accelerate ions locally and open magnetic pathways to the magne­
               tosheath, magnetotail, or solar wind, but it is not always clear whether local energiza­
               tion leads to net atmospheric loss. A central future task is to distinguish between plasma
               redistribution within the Martian system and true escape to interplanetary space, espe­
                 cially for heavy ions.
             The role of heavy ions in modifying reconnection physics requires further study. Plan­
               etary heavy ions can alter Alfvén speeds, current-sheet scales, Hall signatures, outflow
               properties, and energy partition. Existing observations suggest species-dependent accel­
                eration, particularly in the magnetotail, but it remains unclear how heavy ions modify
              the reconnection rate, diffusion-region structure, and coupling between kinetic-scale
              processes and global plasma transport. Realistic multi-fluid, hybrid, and kinetic simula­
               tions with Martian ion composition are needed to address this problem.

Lin et al. Discover Space      (2026) 130:17                                                                       Page 25 of 29



             The transition between collisionless and collisional reconnection in the Martian iono­
              sphere remains poorly constrained. At higher altitudes, reconnection can display Hall-
                 like signatures and jet-like outflows, whereas at lower altitudes ion-neutral collisions
           may suppress or strongly modify reconnection. Future work should clarify whether
             reconnection in the lower ionosphere can still contribute to heating, vertical plasma
               transport, or magnetic topology change even when fast outflows are absent.
             The coupling between IMF variability and local magnetic topology is still not fully
              understood. Recent multipoint observations suggest that IMF rotations can trigger
             reconnection in both the magnetotail and the dayside upper ionosphere. However, the
                relative roles of upstream forcing, internal current-sheet evolution, crustal-field geom­
                 etry, and ionospheric plasma conditions remain  difficult to separate. Coordinated
             upstream and near-Mars measurements will be essential for determining how rapidly
              the Martian plasma environment responds to IMF changes.
             The global spatial scale and system-level influence of Martian reconnection remain
                largely unknown. Most current observations identify reconnection locally, but they do
             not determine how large the reconnection region is, how far reconnection-generated
               structures propagate, or how much of the induced magnetosphere is affected by a given
               event. Future multi-spacecraft observations are therefore crucial [101]. Simultane­
             ous measurements at different locations around Mars could reveal the spatial extent of
             reconnection X-lines, the propagation range of jets, flux ropes, and reconnection fronts,
            and the degree to which local reconnection events reorganize the global induced magne­
               tosphere. Such joint observations would help bridge the gap between local kinetic signa­
               tures and planetary-scale plasma transport.
               Addressing these questions will require a combination of long-term statistical sur­
               veys, coordinated multi-spacecraft observations, and numerical modeling. Global MHD
            and hybrid simulations are needed to capture IMF draping, crustal-field topology, and
              magnetotail reconfiguration, while kinetic simulations are required to resolve diffusion-
              region physics, heavy-ion effects, and collisional processes. Upcoming missions such as
            NASA’s ESCAPADE [111] and JAXA’s Mars Moons eXploration (MMX) [112], together
             with Tianwen-1, Mars Express, and possible next-generation orbiters, will be central to
              building a more complete picture of reconnection at Mars.
