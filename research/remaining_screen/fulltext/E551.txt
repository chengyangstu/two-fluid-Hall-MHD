15    Abstract
16     In the diﬀusion region of magnetotail reconnection, particle distributions are highly struc-
17     tured, exhibiting triangular shapes and multiple striations that deviate dramatically from
18     the Maxwellian distribution. Fully kinetic simulations have been demonstrated to be ca-
19     pable of producing the essential structures of the observed distribution functions, yet are
20     computationally not feasible for 3D global simulations. The ﬂuid models used for large-
21     scale simulations, on the other hand, do not have the kinetic physics necessary for de-
22     scribing reconnection accurately. Our study aims to bridge fully kinetic and ﬂuid sim-
23     ulations by quantifying the information required to capture the non-Maxwellian features
24     in the distributions underlying the closures used in the ﬂuid code. We compare the re-
25     sults of fully kinetic simulations to observed electron velocity distributions in a magne-
26      totail reconnection diﬀusion region, and use the maximum entropy model to reconstruct
27     electron and ion distributions using various numbers of moments obtained from the sim-
28     ulation. Our results indicate that using only local moments, the maximum entropy model
29    can reproduce many of the features of the distributions: (1) the electron-outﬂow distri-
30     bution with a tilted triangular structure is reproduced with 21 or more moments in agree-
31    ment with Ng, Hakim, and Bhattacharjee [2018] and (2) counterstreaming distributions
32    can be captured with the 35-moment model when the separation in velocity space be-
33    tween the populations is large.


34    Plain Language Summary

35          Fluid modelling of plasmas in collisionless environments such as the magnetosphere
36       is challenging because the approximation that collisions are important does not hold. It
37       is important to understand the motion of individual particles as collisions do not bring
38    them back to equilibrium, However, this is computationally intensive and cannot be used
39     to model the Earth at the present time. We use a recent ﬂuid model which is based on
40    maximising the entropy of an underlying distribution of particles and attempt to recon-
41     struct particle distributions during reconnection in the Earth’s magnetotail. We show
42     that most features of the distribution function can be reproduced using an increasing num-
43     ber of ﬂuid moments.


44   1 Introduction

45         Non-Maxwellian particle velocity distributions prevail in weakly collisional plasma
46     environments such as the magnetosphere. In particular, for magnetic reconnection re-
47     gions in the magnetotail, strongly anisotropic electron velocity distributions with p∥>
48    p⊥are found in inﬂow regions [Øieroset, Lin, Phan, Larson, & Bale, 2002], while trian-
49     gular distributions with accelerated electrons are found within the electron diﬀusion re-
50     gion and close to the x-point during antiparallel reconnection [Torbert et al., 2018]. The
51     formation of these distributions can be explained by electron trapping in the inﬂow [Egedal,
52     Le, & Daughton, 2013], while in the electron diﬀusion region, the meandering electron
53     orbits in the magnetic ﬁeld reversal explains the striated and counterstreaming popu-
54     lations [Bessho, Chen, Shuster, & Wang, 2014; Bessho, Chen, Wang, & Hesse, 2018; Fu-
55     jimoto & Sydora, 2009; Ng, Egedal, Le, & Daughton, 2012; Ng, Egedal, Le, Daughton,
56   & Chen, 2011].

57         Although kinetic physics is necessary to capture these structures which are depen-
58     dent on the electron trajectories, fully kinetic simulations are computationally expen-
59      sive, and many global simulations of magnetospheres use ﬂuid models such as magne-
60    tohydrodynamics (MHD). There have been diﬀerent approaches to incorporate physics
61    beyond MHD in global simulations. This includes hybrid-PIC [Karimabadi, Vu, Krauss-
62    Varban, & Omelchenko, 2006; Lu et al., 2015] and Vlasov codes [Kempf et al., 2013; Palm-
63     roth et al., 2018], which simulate kinetic ions and ﬂuid electrons. Another approach in-
64     volves embedding PIC boxes within a larger-scale MHD or two-ﬂuid simulation, so that

 65     only the regions in which kinetic eﬀects are thought to be important use a fully kinetic
 66    model [Fujimoto, 2018; Ho, Datta, & Shumlak, 2018; Lautenbach & Grauer, 2018; T´oth
 67     et al., 2017, 2016].

 68        The approach discussed in this paper involves extended ﬂuid models which evolve
 69     the higher moments of the Vlasov equation have been developed [Hesse, Winske, & Kuznetsova,
 70     1995; Ng, Hakim, Bhattacharjee, Stanier, & Daughton, 2017; Wang, Hakim, Bhattachar-
 71      jee, & Germaschewski, 2015; Yin, Winske, Gary, & Birn, 2001]. In addition to the usual
 72     density and momentum moments, these models evolve the pressure tensor, which is im-
 73     portant for balancing the reconnection electric ﬁeld at the x-point. Observational val-
 74     idation tests of such models have produced encouraging results [Dong et al., 2019; Wang
 75     et al., 2018]. However, closures for general plasma parameters are challenging to develop
 76    and these models do not capture all the kinetic physics, which can aﬀect the reconnec-
 77     tion rate and development of instabilities [Ng et al., 2017; Ng, Hakim, Juno, & Bhattachar-
 78      jee, 2019].

 79          In this paper we focus on what distribution function information can be obtained
 80     given a ﬁxed number of known moments of velocity distribution functions. We use the
 81    maximum entropy closure [Levermore, 1996], which has the useful property that the de-
 82     rived ﬂuid equations are hyperbolic. Our earlier work on using this closure to reconstruct
 83     electron distributions in reconnection regions has shown that the characteristic triangu-
 84      lar shape can be recovered using 21 moments (the heat ﬂux tensor and scalar v4 moment)
 85    [Ng et al., 2018]. In this study, we use parameters more relevant to the event reported
 86    by Torbert et al. [2018], and the distributions in the simulation are chosen to correspond
 87     to the observations. We also increase the number of moments kept to 35, thereby demon-
 88     strating the ability of the model to recover counterstreaming populations.

 89        The remainder of this paper is organised as follows: In Section 2 we review the deriva-
 90     tion of the maximum closure and the ﬂuid equations obtained, compared to the Chapman-
 91    Enskog and Grad approaches. In Section 3 we perform comparisons between the elec-
 92     tron distribution functions obtained from MMS observations, a kinetic simulation and
 93     reconstructions using successively higher numbers of moments. We then discuss the re-
 94     sults in Section 4 and the implications for ﬂuid modelling.


 95   2 Maximum entropy closure

 96       Maximum entropy methods are used to determine probability distributions given
 97     limited information [Jaynes, 1957], and have been used in many scientiﬁc ﬁelds includ-
 98     ing astrophysics [Richstone & Tremaine, 1988], biology [Yeo & Burge, 2004] and natu-
 99      ral language processing [Charniak, 2000]. In the context of ﬂuid dynamics, the maximum
100     entropy ﬂuid closure was ﬁrst derived in [Levermore, 1996] as an alternative to the tra-
101     ditional Chapman-Enskog and Grad approaches [Chapman & Cowling, 1970; Grad, 1949b].
102      It is a non-perturbative closure and is constructed to ensure the positivity of the distri-
103     bution function f and the hyperbolicity of the convective part of the ﬂuid equations ob-
104     tained. Here we brieﬂy review the derivation of the moment equations.

            In contrast to the previous approaches, the maximum entropy approach introduced
      by Levermore [Levermore, 1996] is non-perturbative and uses a distribution function
                                   f(α, v) = exp(ατm(v)),                               (7)

126    where m(v) a vector containing monomials up to a certain degree of the particle veloc-
127      ity components and α is a vector of closure coeﬃcients [Levermore, 1996].

128         For the distribution function to remain ﬁnite, as v →∞, the polynomial in the
129    exponent must be of an even degree [Levermore, 1996]. In three dimensions, the lowest-
130     order solutions have maximum degree 2, leading to the ﬁve- and ten-moment closures,
131     in which the density, momentum and scalar or tensor pressures are evolved. At maxi-
132   mum degree 4, the 14-, 21-, 26- and 35-moment systems are obtained [Levermore, 1996].
133     In this work we use the 5-, 10-, 14-, 21- and 35-moment models. With the 14-moment
134     model, the vector Qi = m R fv2vid3v and the scalar 4th moment R = m R fv4d3v are
135     used. The 21-moment model uses the scalar R as well as the tensor Qijk = m R fvivjvkd3v.
136    The 35-moment model includes the heat-ﬂux tensor but uses a tensor Rijkl = R fvivjvkvld3v
137     instead of a scalar r. By integrating using the deviation from the mean velocities rather
138    than v, information about the heat ﬂux and kurtosis, which provides information about
139     the weight of the tails of the distribution [Westfall, 2014], are obtained.

140        The free parameters in this closure model are the coeﬃcients α, which are calcu-
141     lated by maximising the entropy given the known moments of the distribution. We brieﬂy
142     reproduce the derivation [Levermore, 1996] here.

158   3 Reconstruction of distributions in reconnection regions

159         During the Magnetospheric Multiscale (MMS) encounter with an electron diﬀu-
160     sion region described in [Torbert et al., 2018], highly non-Maxwellian electron distribu-
161     tion functions were observed close to the x-point. These velocity distributions have been
162     studied in detail [Bessho et al., 2018] and it has been shown that the combination of the
163     electron meandering motion and reconnection electric ﬁeld is important for setting the
164     structure of the distribution functions. These distributions are used to identify regions
165     in the simulation for study and provide a link to reality.

166       We focus on understanding what aspects of the distribution function in this recon-
167     nection region successively higher moment models can capture compared to kinetic sim-
168     ulations. To that end, we use the three distributions corresponding to the inﬂow, below
169     the x-point and exhaust regions shown in Fig. 1, and perform fully kinetic particle-in-
170      cell simulations for comparison. The known moments are then used to reconstruct dis-
171     tributions using the maximum-entropy model for various numbers of moments. Aside
172    from the usual isotropic Maxwellian, we use the 10-, 14-, 21- and 35-moment models, which
173    have important properties in ﬂuid simulations. The 10-moment model is being used in
174     global simulations of magnetospheres [Dong et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018] and includes
175     the pressure tensor, which is important for balancing the reconnection electric ﬁeld in
176      collisionless reconnection. The 14- and 21-moment models include the heat-ﬂux vector
177    and tensor respectively, and it has been shown that capturing the heat ﬂux correctly is
178     important for reproducing the reconnection rate observed in kinetic simulations [Allmann-
179    Rahn, Trost, & Grauer, 2018; Ng et al., 2017]. The 35-moment model extends our pre-
180     vious study [Ng et al., 2018] by including the tensor vvvv moment instead of a scalar
181     v4.

188        The simulations are performed using the particle-in-cell code PSC [Germaschewski
189     et al., 2016]. The initial condition is a Harris sheet [Harris, 1962], with mi/me = 100,
190     ωpe/ωce = 2, Ti/Te = 5 with a background density nb/n0 = 0.05. We use the LMN
191     coordinate system, where L is in the outﬂow direction, M is out-of-plane and N is in the
192    inﬂow direction, and the computational domain is LL ×LN = 51.2di ×12.8di resolved
193    by 2048×512 cells. The time step is ∆t = 0.17ω−1pe . Reconnection is initiated by a small
194    magnetic ﬁeld perturbation. Particles from the kinetic simulations are chosen from re-
195     gions corresponding to the observed distributions. We sample the particles from boxes
196     with side 3.0de = 0.3di around the points marked in Fig. 1 at tωci = 16. There are
197     approximately 3500 particles in the inﬂow sample and more than 5000 particles in the
198     x-point and exhaust samples.

202     functions presented here are in p-space. However, the closure calculations remain the
203    same as the model in Eq. (7) is non-relativistic, and we simply use p = mev, with me =
204    1 in our simulations. We note that this method reconstructs the distribution function

287   4 Conclusion

288       We have used successively higher numbers of moments to reconstruct electron ve-
289     locity distributions in reconnection regions. The maximum entropy method, which we
290     use, has the properties of guaranteeing the positivity of the resulting distribution and
291     the hyperbolicity of the ﬂuid equations obtained, but is more diﬃcult to evaluate as no
292     closed form solutions exist for > 10 moments. This closure reconstructs the distribu-
293     tion from only the locally known moments without information about the electromag-
294     netic ﬁelds or physical processes, allowing it to be used for diﬀerent plasma parameters.

316     lems Miller and Shumlak [2016], but still requires study for physics relevant to the mag-
317     netosphere. For the maximum-entropy model, each timestep would involve solving an
318     optimisation problem for the closure parameters, which is challenging and computation-
